Gradient Descent

A 3-minute lesson · from your ML-math wiki (wiki/concepts/gradient-descent.md) · source: Mathematics for Machine Learning §7.1

One-liner: To find the bottom of a valley you can't see, keep taking small steps in whatever direction is most steeply downhill from where you stand.

🟢 Intuition The foggy hillside

You're on a hillside in thick fog and want the lowest point. You can't see far — but under your feet you can feel which way the ground tilts. So you step downhill, feel again, step again. When the ground goes flat, you've hit a valley bottom.

That's the whole algorithm. The landscape is the model's error; "training" is rolling a ball to the lowest error. Two catches that haunt all of ML:

  • You only reach your valley. If the terrain has several dips, you land in whichever one you started above — not necessarily the deepest.
  • Step size is everything. Tiny steps take forever; giant steps fling you across the valley and out the other side.

See it. A ball descends the error curve f(x). Drag the learning rate and watch the failure modes appear — too small crawls, too big oscillates or diverges.

🟡 Mechanics The update rule

We want \(\min_{\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x})\) for a differentiable \(f:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}\). The gradient \(\nabla f\) points in the direction of steepest ascent — so we step the opposite way:

$$ \mathbf{x}_{i+1} = \mathbf{x}_i - \gamma_i\,\big((\nabla f)(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)^\top $$

For a small enough step-size \(\gamma_i \ge 0\), each step doesn't increase the error: \(f(\mathbf{x}_0)\ge f(\mathbf{x}_1)\ge\cdots\), converging to a local minimum. The step-size \(\gamma\) is the learning rate you tune in every training script.

Momentum gives the ball memory, so it stops pinballing between valley walls and averages out noise:

$$ \mathbf{x}_{i+1} = \mathbf{x}_i - \gamma_i\big((\nabla f)(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)^\top + \alpha\,\Delta\mathbf{x}_i,\qquad \Delta\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_{i-1} $$

In ML: the objective is the loss, the parameters are the weights. For big data you sample a few points per step → stochastic gradient descent.

🔴 Theory Why it zigzags

Gradient descent is first-order — it feels the slope but ignores the curvature. On a long, thin valley the gradient points nearly across the valley rather than toward the bottom, so the path zigzags. The convergence speed is governed by the condition number

$$ \kappa = \frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)} $$

the ratio of the most- to least-curved direction (largest over smallest singular value). Big \(\kappa\) = slow, zigzagging descent. Fixes: preconditioning (reshape the bowl), momentum, or going second-order — Newton / L-BFGS use the Hessian; conjugate gradient reuses past directions. And for a convex \(f\), every local minimum is the global one — the starting-point lottery disappears entirely.

The 30-second recap

  • What: step opposite the gradient, repeat, until flat.
  • The one knob: learning rate γ — too small crawls, too big diverges.
  • The trap: only finds a local min (unless the problem is convex).
  • The weakness: zigzags on ill-conditioned (κ ≫ 1) problems; momentum / 2nd-order methods fix it.
