Singular Value Decomposition

Every matrix is secretly just: rotate → stretch → rotate. The formulas below actually render.

🟢 INTUITION

Every matrix has no exceptions

Take any matrix $A$ — square, rectangular, ugly, it doesn't matter. Whatever transformation it performs can always be broken into three clean moves, in order:

🔄

1. Spin the input

$V^\top$ rotates your input to line up with a good set of perpendicular axes.

↔️

2. Stretch each axis

$\Sigma$ stretches along each axis by a fixed positive amount (and may drop dimensions).

🔄

3. Spin the output

$U$ rotates the stretched result into the output space.

That is the whole decomposition:

$$A = U\,\Sigma\,V^\top$$

$U$ and $V$ are pure rotations (no distortion). $\Sigma$ in the middle is just a list of positive stretch amounts. Because this works for every matrix and always exists, it has been called the fundamental theorem of linear algebra.

🟡 MECHANICS

The pieces, named

Theorem 4.22: every matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ of rank $r$ factorizes as

$$A = U\,\Sigma\,V^\top$$
PieceShapeWhat it is
$U$$m\times m$ orthogonalcolumns $\mathbf{u}_i$ = left-singular vectors (output rotation)
$V$$n\times n$ orthogonalcolumns $\mathbf{v}_j$ = right-singular vectors (input rotation)
$\Sigma$$m\times n$ "diagonal"$\Sigma_{ii}=\sigma_i\ge 0$ = singular values (the stretches)

The singular values come out sorted biggest to smallest:

$$\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$$
The key equation, in words: applying the matrix to the $i$-th input direction gives the $i$-th singular value times the $i$-th output direction — $$A\mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i\,\mathbf{u}_i$$ Like the eigenvalue equation, but the input vector $\mathbf{v}_i$ and the output vector $\mathbf{u}_i$ are allowed to differ.

How you compute it

  • Diagonalize the symmetric matrix $A^\top A = P D P^\top$. Then $V = P$.
  • The stretches are the square roots of those eigenvalues: $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$.
  • Recover the output directions by normalizing the images: $\mathbf{u}_i = \tfrac{1}{\sigma_i}A\mathbf{v}_i$.

(This $A^\top A$ route is great for understanding but numerically poor — real libraries use dedicated SVD algorithms.)

🟡 WORKED EXAMPLE

A concrete 2×3 matrix (Example 4.13)

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

Form $A^\top A$ and find its eigenvalues: $6,\ 1,\ 0$. So:

$$\sigma_1 = \sqrt{6}, \qquad \sigma_2 = 1, \qquad (\text{rank } 2)$$ $$\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{6} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad U = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

The third direction has a zero singular value — that's the null space, the direction $A$ collapses to nothing.

🔴 WHY ML CARES

"Keep the big stretches, drop the small ones"

Because the singular values are ranked by importance (biggest stretch = direction carrying the most information), you can throw away the small ones and keep a faithful, compressed copy. That single idea is:

  • PCA — the biggest-stretch directions are exactly the directions your data spreads out in most.
  • Recommender systems — factor a ratings matrix; the $\mathbf{u}_i$ become "stereotypical movies," the $\mathbf{v}_j$ become "stereotypical viewers."
  • Image compression, topic modeling, least squares (the pseudo-inverse) — all the same truncation trick.
SVD vs. eigendecomposition. Eigendecomposition only works on nice square matrices, and its axes need not be perpendicular. SVD works on anything, and $U,V$ are always true rotations. The price: input and output directions differ, so you need two sets of rotation vectors instead of one. For a symmetric positive-definite matrix the two coincide and the SVD equals the eigendecomposition.
⏱️ 10-second takeaway: Every matrix = rotate, stretch, rotate ($A = U\Sigma V^\top$). The stretches come ranked by importance, so keeping the big ones and dropping the small ones is the universal compression / feature-extraction move — and that's literally what PCA, recommenders, and least-squares all are underneath.
