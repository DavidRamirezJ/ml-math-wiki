Numbers → dot products → one neuron · the first three building blocks for MLP vs CNN · 2026-10-13
Here's the whole story in three sentences. (1) A neural network only ever sees lists and grids of numbers. (2) Its one core move is the dot product: multiply two lists number by number, then add everything up. (3) A neuron is just a dot product, plus a small extra number, passed through a simple "squash" function.

1Data as numbers: vectors, matrices, tensors

Before a network can learn anything, everything has to become numbers. There are only three shapes you need to know, and each one is just the previous one with one more direction.

Vector — a list

A vector is an ordered list of numbers. For example, a house described by size, rooms and age:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 120 \\ 3 \\ 15 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$$
$\mathbf{x}$
bold lowercase letter = a vector
$x_1, x_2, x_3$
its entries: $x_1 = 120$, $x_2 = 3$, $x_3 = 15$. The small number underneath is the position.
$\in$
"is a member of" / "lives in"
$\mathbb{R}^{3}$
the set of all lists of 3 real numbers

The order matters: slot 1 is always size, slot 2 is always rooms. Swap them and it's a different house.

Matrix — a grid

A matrix is a grid of numbers with rows and columns. A tiny 2×3 example:

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 3}$$
$\mathbf{A}$
bold capital letter = a matrix
$2\times 3$
2 rows, 3 columns — always rows first
$A_{ij}$
the entry in row $i$, column $j$. Here $A_{23} = 6$ (row 2, column 3).

You can see a matrix as a stack of row vectors, or as columns side by side. Both views are useful later.

Try it: a grayscale image is a matrix

Click the pixels to change their brightness (0 = black, 9 = white). Watch the matrix of numbers underneath change. That matrix is exactly what the network receives.

A colour image has 3 of these grids stacked: one for red, one for green, one for blue. A stack of matrices is a tensor.

Tensor — a stack of grids

A tensor is the general word for "a box of numbers with any number of directions". Its shape tells you the size in each direction.

ObjectDirectionsExample shapeExample
number (scalar)0$()$a temperature: 21
vector1$(3)$the house above
matrix2$(28, 28)$a grayscale digit image
3-D tensor3$(3, 224, 224)$a colour photo: channels × height × width

🔗 Why this matters for MLP vs CNN: an MLP needs a vector, so it flattens the $(28,28)$ image into a $(784)$ list and loses the layout. A CNN keeps the matrix / tensor shape, so it knows which pixels are neighbours.

1bA closer look: what exactly is a tensor?

A tensor is just a box of numbers arranged along some number of directions. Each direction is called an axis. The number of axes tells you how many indices you need to point at one single number.

Think about how much information you need to find one seat:

The shape lists the size of each axis, in order. The total count is all the sizes multiplied together:

$$\text{shape } (d_1, d_2, \dots, d_k) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{total} = d_1 \times d_2 \times \dots \times d_k$$
$k$

🖼️ Why a colour photo is a 3-D tensor

A grayscale photo has one brightness value per pixel, so it's a grid: shape $(H, W)$. A colour photo has three values per pixel (red, green and blue), so it's three grids stacked on top of each other: shape $(3, H, W)$. Training uses many photos at once, a batch, which adds a fourth axis in front: $(32, 3, 224, 224)$ ≈ 4.8 million numbers.

So X[0, 10, 20] means: channel 0 (red), row 10, column 20.

Same numbers, different shape: reshape and flatten

The same 12 numbers can be arranged in many shapes. Changing the arrangement is called reshaping. Nothing is added or lost; the numbers just sit in a different layout. Flattening means reshaping into one long list. That's exactly what an MLP does to an image.

A small warning: "tensor" in physics vs in ML

In physics and pure math, a tensor is something deeper: an object that changes in a specific way when you change coordinates. In machine learning and PyTorch, the word simply means a multi-dimensional array of numbers. For this wiki and the LLM book, use the ML meaning.

2The core operation: the dot product

If you only remember one thing from this page, make it this. The dot product takes two lists of the same length, multiplies them slot by slot, and adds the results into one number.

$$\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\,x_i \;=\; w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + \dots + w_nx_n$$
$\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}$
"w dot x" — the dot product of the two vectors
$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$
the big Greek S (sigma) means "add up". The bottom $i=1$ says start at slot 1; the top $n$ says stop at slot $n$.
$w_i\,x_i$
multiply slot $i$ of $\mathbf{w}$ by slot $i$ of $\mathbf{x}$

Try it: drag the weights

Think of $\mathbf{x}$ as a recipe's ingredients and $\mathbf{w}$ as how much you care about each one. The dot product is the overall score.

The geometric meaning (how aligned are two arrows?)

There's a second way to see the same number:

$$\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} = \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\cos\theta$$
$\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert$
the length of the arrow $\mathbf{w}$
$\theta$
the angle between the two arrows
$\cos\theta$
1 when they point the same way, 0 at a right angle, −1 when opposite

So the dot product is big when the two arrows point the same way. That's why a neuron, or a CNN filter, "fires" when the input looks like its weights: it is a pattern-matcher.

Matrix multiplication = many dot products at once

Multiplying a matrix by a vector just does one dot product per row. Each row of $\mathbf{W}$ is one "scoring recipe".

$$\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ -1 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\cdot2 + 0\cdot1 + 2\cdot3 \\ -1\cdot2 + 3\cdot1 + 1\cdot3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$$
$(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x})_i = \sum_j W_{ij}\,x_j$
entry $i$ of the result = row $i$ of $\mathbf{W}$ dotted with $\mathbf{x}$
shapes
$(2\times3)$ times $(3)$ gives $(2)$. The inner sizes (3 and 3) must match; the outer size (2) is what you get.

A matrix is a machine that moves space (a linear map)

Here's the deeper view. A 2×2 matrix takes every point of the plane and sends it somewhere new. It can stretch, squash, rotate or slant the plane, but straight lines stay straight and the origin stays put. That's what linear means.

$$\mathbf{W} = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$$

Faint grid = before. Coloured shape = after. The blue arrow is where $(1,0)$ lands (the first column); the orange arrow is where $(0,1)$ lands (the second column).

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}\qquad f(a\mathbf{u} + b\mathbf{v}) = a\,f(\mathbf{u}) + b\,f(\mathbf{v})$$

The second rule reads: "transforming a mix is the same as mixing the transforms". That's the definition of a linear map.

3One neuron, then one layer

Now we can build a neuron. It takes three simple steps:

  1. Weigh: take the dot product of the inputs with the weights.
  2. Shift: add one extra number, the bias, which moves the threshold up or down.
  3. Squash: pass the result through an activation function, such as ReLU.
$$z = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + b \qquad\qquad h = \sigma(z)$$
$z$
the raw score before the activation (people call it the "pre-activation")
$b$
the bias: one number per neuron, learned like the weights
$\sigma$
the activation function (Greek letter sigma, lowercase)
$h$
the neuron's output
$\mathrm{ReLU}(z)=\max(0,z)$
the most popular activation: keep positive numbers, turn negatives into 0

A layer = many neurons = a matrix

Put $m$ neurons side by side. Each has its own weight row and its own bias. Stack the rows into a matrix and you get the formula from the MLP page:

$$\mathbf{h} = \sigma(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b})$$
row $i$ of $\mathbf{W}$
the weights of neuron $i$
$\mathbf{b}$
all the biases in one vector
$\sigma(\cdot)$
applied to each entry separately

Why do we need the activation at all?

Without it, stacking layers is pointless. Two linear layers in a row collapse into one:

$$\mathbf{W}_2(\mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{x}) = (\mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{W}_1)\,\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{W}'\mathbf{x}$$

A hundred linear layers are still just one matrix: one stretch-and-rotate of space. They can only draw straight lines. The activation bends the space between layers. That bending is what lets a deep network learn curved, complicated patterns.

🎞️ Animation: watch the activation build a curve

Here's the clearest way to see it. We want a tiny network to draw the dashed curve. The network has one hidden layer. Each hidden neuron computes $\mathrm{ReLU}(x - t)$: it stays flat at 0, then bends at its own point $t$ and goes up. The output adds the neurons together, each one scaled by an output weight $c$:

$$y(x) = a + s\,x + \sum_{k=1}^{N} c_k\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x - t_k)$$
$N$
the number of hidden neurons (use the slider)
$t_k$
where neuron $k$ bends (its bias, with a minus sign)
$c_k$
how strongly neuron $k$ counts in the output (its output weight)
$a + s\,x$
a plain straight line that the bends are added to

Legend: dashed = target curve · thin coloured = each neuron's own contribution · thick = the network's output.

Now untick "use ReLU" and press Play again. Without the activation, each neuron is just $c_k(x - t_k)$: a straight line. And a sum of straight lines is always one straight line, however many neurons you add. The network can never bend. With ReLU, every neuron adds one bend in its own spot, and with enough bends you can draw almost any curve. That's the real job of an activation function: it lets the network bend.

🔗 Back to MLP vs CNN: an MLP layer is exactly $\sigma(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})$ with every entry of $\mathbf{W}$ free. A CNN layer is the same formula, but each neuron only dots a small patch, and every neuron shares the same few weights.

In summary

  • Step 1: networks see vectors (lists), matrices (grids) and tensors (stacks of grids). The shape tells you the size in each direction.
  • Step 2: the dot product multiplies slot by slot and adds up. It's big when the input looks like the weights. Matrix multiplication is one dot product per row, and it moves space in a straight-line way.
  • Tensors: a tensor is a box of numbers with $k$ axes. You need one index per axis to pick a number; the shape lists each axis size, and their product is the total count. Reshape rearranges the same numbers; flatten turns them into one list.
  • Step 3: a neuron = dot product + bias + activation. A layer is many neurons, written $\sigma(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})$. The activation is what stops the layers from collapsing into one — it is what lets the network bend: each ReLU neuron adds one bend, and without it any number of neurons still draws a straight line.
