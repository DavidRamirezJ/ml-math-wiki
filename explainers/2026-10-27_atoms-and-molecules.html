So you don't need to learn 278 pages. Learn the atoms well, learn how they bond, and the rest is assembly.

Most important of all: the 8 principles in section 4 — and under them, the 5 axioms in section 3. If you forget a formula, you can derive it again from them.

2. Why this split? (how the layers were found)

Every concept page in the wiki has a line called “Builds on”, which lists its prerequisites. I followed those links across all 278 pages. For each page, I counted how many other pages depend on it, directly or through a chain. I call that number the page's reach.

The rule I used:

  • An atom has a very high reach, and it needs only other atoms (or nothing).
  • A molecule joins 2 to 4 atoms into one named idea with one formula, and many methods reuse it.
  • A compound is a complete ML method or model, built from several molecules.
A caution about the numbers

The reach numbers come from the links the wiki pages declare. They are a good guide, not a law of nature. For example, “loss function” lists only “vector” as a prerequisite, so it looks more basic than it is. The atom list also uses judgment: exp/log and activation functions are atoms here because softmax, cross-entropy and every neural network need them, even though their pages sit lower in the graph.

3. Validation — is this the most elemental?

Short answer: no, not quite. The 8 principles below are good working rules, but 4 of them can be derived from the others. The real bottom is 5 axioms.

The test: a principle is elemental only if you cannot derive it from the others.

AxiomStatementWhy nothing else gives it
A. LinearityVectors can be added and scaled. A linear map keeps that: $f(ax+by)=af(x)+bf(y)$The base structure. Everything is built on it.
B. Inner product$\langle x,y\rangle$ is symmetric, linear, and $\langle x,x\rangle>0$ when $x\neq 0$A vector space has no length or angle until you add this.
C. Local linearity$f(x+h)\approx f(x)+\nabla f(x)^{\top}h$ for small $h$It needs limits, which A and B do not give.
D. Probability rules$p\ge 0$, total $=1$; sum rule $p(x)=\sum_y p(x,y)$; product rule $p(x,y)=p(y\mid x)\,p(x)$These are the axioms of probability.
E. Learning = optimizationChoose the parameters $\theta$ that make a loss on the data as small as possibleA choice that ML makes, not a theorem.
$\langle x,y\rangle$
the inner product of $x$ and $y$ (for lists of numbers: $x^\top y=\sum_i x_i y_i$)
$\approx$
“is approximately equal to”
$\nabla f(x)^{\top}h$
the gradient of $f$ at $x$, dotted with the small step $h$: the predicted change in $f$
$\sum_y p(x,y)$
add the joint probability over every value of $y$ — this “forgets” $y$
$p(y\mid x)$
the probability of $y$ when you already know $x$

Below the 5 axioms is the floor of school math, which I assume you have: numbers, functions, the $\sum$ sign, limits, and exp/log.

Which of the 8 principles are derived?

PrincipleStatusHow to derive it
1. Add and scale✅ elemental (= A)—
2. Inner product✅ elemental (= B)—
3. Chain rule❌ derived from CLinearize twice: $g(f(x+h))\approx g(f(x)+f'h)\approx g(f(x))+g'\,f'\,h$. The slopes multiply.
4. Step downhill❌ derived from C + B$f$ changes by about $\nabla f^{\top}h$. For a step of fixed length, the inner product is most negative when $h$ points along $-\nabla f$. At a minimum no step helps, so $\nabla f=0$.
5. Probability rules✅ elemental (= D), but the sum rule was missingThe sum rule gives the bottom of Bayes' theorem, $p(x)$, and every mixture model.
6. log: products → sums⚪ floor (a math fact)It explains why we take logs: independent data give a product of probabilities (D).
7. Center and spread❌ derived from A + B + DThe mean is a probability-weighted sum $\sum_x p(x)\,x$; the variance is the expected squared length $\mathbb E\,\lVert x-\mu\rVert^2$.
8. Stretch directions❌ derived from A + B + CMaximize $w^{\top}Sw$ with $\lVert w\rVert=1$. A Lagrange multiplier gives $Sw=\lambda w$.
Are the 15 atoms elemental?

They are the right units to study, but not all of them are irreducible.

  • Irreducible: vector / vector space (A), linear mapping (A), inner product (B), derivative (C), probability distribution (D), loss function (E).
  • Derived, but kept as atoms for study: matrix (the numbers that describe a linear map), norm ($\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}$), basis & rank, gradient, chain rule, random variable, expected value, variance & covariance.
  • Activation function: the need for one is derived — a stack of linear maps is still one linear map (A) — but the exact choice (ReLU, GELU) is a design decision.

4. The 8 working principles — rebuild anything you forget

You don't need to remember formulas. You need to remember 8 principles. If you forget a formula, start from its principle and derive it again in a few lines. These are the most important ideas on the whole page.

#Principle (remember this)What you can derive from it
1Add and scale. Data are vectors; mix them with weights: $\sum_i w_i x_i$.matrix, matrix multiplication, linear layer, embedding lookup
2Inner product = similarity. $x^\top y$ is big when $x$ and $y$ point the same way.length (norm), angle, cosine similarity, projection, attention scores
3Slopes multiply along a chain. $\frac{dL}{dx}=\frac{dL}{dy}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}$gradient, Jacobian, backpropagation
4Step downhill. At a minimum the slope is 0; to get there, step against the gradient.gradient descent, SGD, Adam, closed-form least squares
5Probabilities are ≥ 0 and add up to 1, and $p(a,b)=p(a\mid b)\,p(b)$.softmax, Bayes' theorem, marginals, mixtures
6log turns products into sums. $\log(ab)=\log a+\log b$log-likelihood, MLE, cross-entropy, perplexity
7Center and spread. mean $\mu$, variance $\sigma^2$; standardize with $(x-\mu)/\sigma$.covariance matrix, z-score, layer norm, batch norm, Gaussian
8Find the directions a matrix only stretches. $Av=\lambda v$eigendecomposition, SVD, PCA, low-rank (LoRA)

Practice: re-derive from the principles

Each box shows how to rebuild a formula you might forget. Try it yourself first, then open the box.

Forgot softmax? → principles 5 + 6
  1. You have raw scores $z_i$ (any sign). You want probabilities.
  2. Probabilities must be ≥ 0, so make every score positive: $e^{z_i}$.
  3. Probabilities must add up to 1, so divide by the total: $$p_i=\frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}$$
Forgot cross-entropy? → principles 5 + 6 + 4
  1. Good model = gives high probability to the data: maximize $\prod_n p(y_n)$ (maximum likelihood).
  2. A product of tiny numbers is hard. Take the log (principle 6): maximize $\sum_n \log p(y_n)$.
  3. We like to minimize a loss, so flip the sign: $$L=-\sum_n \log p(y_n)$$ For one example, that is $-\log p_y$.
Forgot Bayes' theorem? → principle 5
  1. Write the joint probability two ways: $p(\theta,x)=p(\theta\mid x)\,p(x)=p(x\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)$.
  2. Divide both sides by $p(x)$: $$p(\theta\mid x)=\frac{p(x\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(x)}$$
Forgot backpropagation? → principle 3
  1. A network is a chain: $x\to h=\sigma(Wx)\to L$.
  2. Multiply the slopes along the chain: $\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial h}\cdot\frac{\partial h}{\partial W}$.
  3. Compute $\frac{\partial L}{\partial h}$ once at the top, and reuse it for every layer below. That reuse is backpropagation.
Forgot the least-squares solution of linear regression? → principles 1 + 4
  1. Predictions are $X\theta$ (principle 1). Loss: $L=\|y-X\theta\|^2$.
  2. At the minimum the gradient is 0 (principle 4): $\nabla_\theta L=-2X^\top(y-X\theta)=0$.
  3. Rearrange: $$X^\top X\,\theta=X^\top y\quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$$
Forgot PCA? → principles 7 + 8 (+ 4)
  1. Center the data and make the covariance matrix $S$ (principle 7).
  2. We want the direction $w$ (length 1) where the projected data spreads the most: maximize $w^\top S w$ with $\|w\|=1$.
  3. Set the gradient of $w^\top S w-\lambda(w^\top w-1)$ to 0 (a Lagrange multiplier): $2Sw-2\lambda w=0$, so $$Sw=\lambda w$$ The best direction is the top eigenvector (principle 8), and its variance is $\lambda$.
Forgot attention? → principles 2 + 5 + 1
  1. Score how much token $i$ matches token $j$ with an inner product: $q_i^\top k_j$ (principle 2).
  2. Turn the scores into weights that are ≥ 0 and add up to 1 with softmax (principle 5). Divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$ first so the scores do not get too big.
  3. Mix the values with those weights (principle 1): $$\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$$
Forgot layer normalization? → principle 7
  1. It is just a z-score on each token's vector: $\hat x=(x-\mu)/\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}$.
  2. Then let the model re-scale and shift it with 2 learned vectors: $y=\gamma\odot\hat x+\beta$.

7. Quick check

In summary

  • Bottom line: about 15 atoms → about 16 molecules → every ML method. Nothing else is needed.
  • Atoms come from 4 pillars + nonlinearity: vectors and matrices, derivatives and the chain rule, random variables and distributions, the loss function, and exp/log plus activation functions.
  • Molecules are the reusable formulas: matrix multiplication, the linear layer, softmax, cross-entropy, gradient descent, backpropagation, eigen/SVD, Gaussian, Bayes, MLE, attention…
  • Validated bottom: 5 axioms — linearity, inner product, local linearity, the probability rules (sum + product), and learning = optimization. Chain rule, gradient descent, mean/variance and eigenvectors are derived from them.
  • The 8 principles are what to keep in your head: add & scale, inner product = similarity, slopes multiply, step downhill, probabilities sum to 1, log turns products into sums, center & spread, directions a matrix only stretches.
  • Next step: learn the atoms first, then the molecules in the order above. Before you read a big method, try to take it apart into molecules yourself.
