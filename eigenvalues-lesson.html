Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors

A 3-minute lesson · from your ML-math wiki (wiki/concepts/eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors.md) · source: Mathematics for Machine Learning §4.2

One-liner: an eigenvector is a special direction a matrix only stretches or shrinks (never knocks off its line), and its eigenvalue is the stretch factor.

🟢 Intuition The matrix's "magic directions"

A matrix usually does something messy to a vector: it rotates, shears, and stretches all at once. But almost every matrix has a few magic directions where the effect is boring — the vector just gets longer or shorter while still pointing along the same line.

Those directions are the eigenvectors; the amount of stretch is the eigenvalue (eigen = German for "own / characteristic"). If λ = 2 that direction doubles; λ = 0.5 halves it; λ < 0 flips it; λ = 0 crushes it to nothing.

See it. The blue arrow is a vector x you drag. The red arrow is Ax — what the matrix does to it. On most directions they point differently; rotate x until red and blue line up — that's an eigenvector, and the length ratio is its eigenvalue.

🟡 Mechanics The eigenvalue equation

For a square matrix \(A\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}\), a scalar \(\lambda\) and nonzero vector \(\mathbf x\) are an eigenvalue / eigenvector pair when:

$$ A\mathbf x = \lambda\mathbf x $$

How to find them:

  • Eigenvalues: solve \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\) — the roots of the characteristic polynomial.
  • Eigenvectors: for each \(\lambda\), solve \((A-\lambda I)\mathbf x=\mathbf 0\).

Worked example. The demo above uses \(A=\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\1&3\end{pmatrix}\). Its characteristic polynomial factors as \((\lambda-2)(\lambda-5)\), so:

$$ \lambda_1=5,\ \mathbf x_1=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}\qquad \lambda_2=2,\ \mathbf x_2=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix} $$

Two free clues: \(\det A=\prod_i\lambda_i\) (here \(10=5\cdot2\)) and \(\operatorname{tr}A=\sum_i\lambda_i\) (here \(7=5+2\)).

🔴 Theory Why ML cares

  • Basis-invariant fingerprint. Similar matrices share eigenvalues, so the spectrum describes the underlying linear map, not the coordinates you happened to pick.
  • Diagonalization. Eigenvectors of \(n\) distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent → they form a basis, and \(A\) becomes a pure stretch in those coordinates (the eigendecomposition). A defective matrix with too few eigenvectors can't be diagonalized.
  • Symmetric case. Any symmetric \(A\) has an orthonormal eigenbasis with real eigenvalues (the spectral theorem) — and \(A^\top A\) is always symmetric positive semidefinite, the foundation of the SVD.

Where it shows up: the principal directions in PCA are eigenvectors of the covariance matrix; Google's PageRank is the dominant eigenvector of a transition matrix; the eigenvalues of the Hessian tell you whether an optimization landscape curves up (min), down (max), or is a saddle.

The 30-second recap

  • What: directions where \(A\mathbf x=\lambda\mathbf x\) — matrix acts as pure scaling.
  • Find: eigenvalues from \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\), eigenvectors from the null space.
  • Shortcut: eigenvalues sum to the trace, multiply to the determinant.
  • Why it matters: the engine under PCA, SVD, PageRank, and Hessian/stability analysis.
