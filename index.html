01

Vector

A vector is anything you can add and scale; in practice it is a list of numbers, and every data point and every neuron in ML is built from it.

Needs: only school arithmetic · Axiom: A — linearity

1 · The big idea

A machine learning model never sees a photo, a word, or a customer directly. It sees a list of numbers. A photo becomes its pixel brightness values. A customer becomes age, income, and number of purchases. That list of numbers is called a vector. Each number in the list is called a component.

A vector has three faces. The first face is an arrow: start at the origin, walk 3 steps right and 2 steps up, and the arrow to that point is the vector (3, 2). The second face is a list: the same arrow written as the two numbers 3 and 2. The arrow and the list carry exactly the same information. The third face is the deepest one: a vector is anything you can add to another thing of its kind, and scale by a number, and still get the same kind of thing back.

Think of adding as two walks in a row. You walk along the first vector, then you walk along the second one from where you stopped. Think of scaling as a zoom on one arrow: it can stretch it, shrink it, or flip it around. These two moves look simple, and they are. But matrices, gradients, and attention are all built from them. A single neuron already uses both moves: it scales each input by a weight and adds the results.

2 · The formal definition

Adding and scaling both work component by component. First with first, second with second.

$$ u + v = (u_1 + v_1,\; u_2 + v_2) \qquad c\,u = (c\,u_1,\; c\,u_2) $$
\(u, v\)
"u", "v" — two vectors of the same size (here, two components each).
\(u_1, u_2\)
"u one", "u two" — the first and second components of u. The small number below is the position in the list.
\(c\)
"c" — a scalar: an ordinary single number. It is called a scalar because it scales things.
\(c\,u\)
"c times u" — multiply every component of u by c.

Now combine the two moves. Scale some vectors, then add them. The result is a linear combination:

$$ a\,u + b\,v $$
\(a, b\)
"a", "b" — two scalars, the weights. You choose them.
\(a\,u + b\,v\)
"a u plus b v" — scale u by a, scale v by b, then add the two results.

All the points you can reach with linear combinations of a set of vectors form the span of that set. Finally, a vector space is a collection where adding two members, or scaling a member, never takes you outside the collection. The moves also follow a few common-sense rules:

$$ u + v = v + u, \qquad u + 0 = u, \qquad u + (-u) = 0, \qquad a\,(u + v) = a\,u + a\,v $$
\(u + v = v + u\)
"u plus v equals v plus u" — the order of adding does not matter.
\(0\)
"the zero vector" — the vector with all components zero. Adding it changes nothing.
\(-u\)
"minus u" — the opposite of u. It cancels u and gives the zero vector.
\(a(u+v)\)
"a times the sum u plus v" — scaling spreads over a sum: it equals a u plus a v.

3 · Worked example 1

Add, scale, and combine with u = (3, 1) and v = (1, 2).

Step 1, add. Write each vector as a column and add component by component:

$$ u+v=\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}4\\3\end{bmatrix} $$

In the picture, you put the tail of \(v\) on the tip of \(u\), and you end at \((4,3)\).

Step 2, check the order — addition does not care about order:

$$ v+u=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}4\\3\end{bmatrix} $$

Same point. The two walks trace the two sides of a parallelogram.

Step 3, scale a new vector \(u=(2,1)\). Scaling multiplies every component:

$$ 2\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}4\\2\end{bmatrix},\quad -1\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-2\\-1\end{bmatrix},\quad \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\[2pt]\tfrac12\end{bmatrix},\quad 0\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix} $$

With \(c=-1\) the arrow flips; with \(c=\tfrac12\) it shrinks; with \(c=0\) it collapses to a point.

Step 4, the puzzle from the video. With \(u=(2,1)\) and \(v=(1,2)\), can we reach \((5,4)\)? Try \(a=2,\ b=1\):

$$ 2\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}4\\2\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5\\4\end{bmatrix} $$

Yes — \(2u+v=(5,4)\).

4 · Worked example 2

An ML example: blend two tiny images, then run one neuron.

Part A. Take two toy images of 2×2 pixels. Each pixel is a brightness from 0 to 1. Write the 4 pixels as a column vector. Blending is a scaled sum of the two columns:

$$ \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}+\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\0.5\\0.5\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.5\\0.5\\0.25\\0.75\end{bmatrix} $$

Each pixel is the average of the two pictures.

Part B. A neuron multiplies each input by a weight and adds them up — that is a dot product \(w^\top x\):

$$ w^\top x=\begin{bmatrix}0.5&-1&2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\3\end{bmatrix}=0.5\cdot2+(-1)\cdot1+2\cdot3=6 $$

A large language model computes billions of these sums.

5 · Common mistakes

Thinking two vectors always span the plane. If they lie on the same line, like (2, 1) and (4, 2), the span is only that line. The second vector is just 2 times the first, so it adds nothing new. Video 03 studies this.

Thinking a vector must be an arrow. Sound waves are vectors too. Add two waves point by point and you get a wave. Multiply a wave by 2 and it is still a wave. Only the two moves matter.

Adding vectors of different sizes. The rule is "first with first, second with second". A vector with 3 components cannot be added to one with 4.

6 · Where it comes from & where ML uses it

The definition "anything you can add and scale" is the first axiom of this series, axiom A: linearity. Anything we prove with only adding and scaling works for arrows, for lists of data, for sound, and for images. The list rule always agrees with the arrow picture, so we can trust it in a hundred dimensions, where no picture exists.

  • Every data point is a vector: a house with 120 m², 3 bedrooms, and 15 years of age is (120, 3, 15).
  • A word becomes a vector of a few hundred learned numbers (a word embedding).
  • An image becomes a vector of pixel brightness values; blending images is a linear combination.
  • Every neuron computes a weighted sum, which is a linear combination of its inputs.
  • Averaging data, mixing sounds, and mixing images are all linear combinations.

7 · Try it yourself

Q1. What is 3·(1, −2) + (2, 5)?

Show the answer

Scale first: 3·(1, −2) = (3, −6). Then add (2, 5): 3 + 2 = 5 and −6 + 5 = −1. The answer is (5, −1).

Q2. Use only the vector (2, 1). Can you reach (6, 3)? Can you reach (1, 1)?

Show the answer

With one vector, a linear combination is just c·(2, 1). (6, 3) = 3·(2, 1), so yes, it is in the span. For (1, 1), the first component needs c = ½. Then the second component is ½, not 1. So (1, 1) is outside the span. One vector only reaches a line.

Q3 (new). Find a and b so that a·(1, 0) + b·(0, 1) = (−4, 7).

Show the answer

a·(1, 0) = (a, 0) and b·(0, 1) = (0, b). Their sum is (a, b). So a = −4 and b = 7.

In summary
  • A vector is anything you can add and scale; in practice it is a list of numbers, and in 2D it is an arrow.
  • You add and scale component by component; order does not matter when you add.
  • Scale and then add, and you get a linear combination; all reachable points form the span.
  • In ML, every data point is a vector and every neuron computes a linear combination.
02

Matrix

A matrix is a table that stores data and a machine that moves vectors: A times x is a linear combination of the columns of A.

Needs: video 01 · Axiom: A — linearity

1 · The big idea

A model almost never looks at one vector alone. It looks at a whole table of vectors at once. A rectangular grid of numbers, with rows and columns, is called a matrix. A matrix with m rows and n columns is an "m by n" matrix. We always say the rows first.

A matrix has two faces. The first face is a table. Stack three houses, one per row: area, bedrooms, age. Each row is one data point. Each column is one feature across all the houses. A batch of training data is stored exactly like this.

The second face is a machine. A matrix takes a vector in and gives a new vector out. The secret is simple: A times x is a linear combination of the columns of A, and the components of x are the weights. So the columns tell you everything. The first column is where the arrow e₁ = (1, 0) lands. The second column is where e₂ = (0, 1) lands. Every layer of a neural network keeps what it has learned in such a machine.

2 · The formal definition

Each entry has an address: first the row, then the column. In the house table, a₂₁ = 80, the area of the second house. Matrix times vector, in the column view:

$$ A\,x = x_1\,\mathbf{a}_1 + x_2\,\mathbf{a}_2 + \dots + x_n\,\mathbf{a}_n $$
\(A\)
"A" — an m by n matrix.
\(x\)
"x" — the input vector. It must have n components, one per column of A.
\(x_j\)
"x j" — the j-th component of x. It is the weight for column j.
\(\mathbf{a}_j\)
"column j of A" — the j-th column, a vector with m components.

Doing one matrix after another is matrix multiplication. Entry (i, j) of the product is row i of A times column j of B:

$$ (AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k} a_{ik}\, b_{kj} $$
\((AB)_{ij}\)
"A B, i j" — the entry of the product in row i, column j.
\(\sum_k\)
"the sum over k" — add up the terms for every value of k.
\(a_{ik}\)
"a i k" — the entry of A in row i, column k.
\(b_{kj}\)
"b k j" — the entry of B in row k, column j. Matching k means "multiply matching entries".

Shape rule: if A is m×n and B is n×p, then AB is m×p. The two inner numbers must match, and they disappear. The order is important: in ABx, B acts first, because B stands next to x. Two more tools:

$$ (A^{\mathsf T})_{ij} = a_{ji} \qquad I\,x = x $$
\(A^{\mathsf T}\)
"A transpose" — the transpose: flip A over its diagonal so rows become columns. An m×n matrix becomes n×m.
\(a_{ji}\)
"a j i" — the entry in row j, column i of A. The two indices swap.
\(I\)
"I" — the identity matrix: ones on the diagonal, zeros everywhere else. It changes nothing, like the number 1.

3 · Worked example 1

A times x, both views, then a full product. \(A\) has columns \((2,1)\) and \((1,3)\), and \(x=(1,2)\).

Column view — \(Ax\) is a weighted combination of the columns:

$$ Ax=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}=1\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}+2\begin{bmatrix}1\\3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}4\\7\end{bmatrix} $$

Row view — each output is one row dotted with \(x\): \(2\cdot1+1\cdot2=4\) and \(1\cdot1+3\cdot2=7\). Same answer. This multiply-and-add pattern is the inner product (video 05).

Now a full product — each entry is one row of the left matrix dotted with one column of the right, and order matters:

$$ AB=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}5&6\\7&8\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}19&22\\43&50\end{bmatrix},\qquad BA=\begin{bmatrix}5&6\\7&8\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}23&34\\31&46\end{bmatrix} $$

\(AB\neq BA\) — swapping the order gives a different matrix.

4 · Worked example 2

An ML example: score three houses with one product. The data matrix \(X\) has one house per row; the toy model weights are \(w=(2,10,-1)\):

$$ Xw=\begin{bmatrix}120&3&15\\80&2&30\\150&4&5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2\\10\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}255\\150\\335\end{bmatrix} $$

One matrix product gives three predictions, one per row. A neural-network layer works the same way: a layer with 3 inputs and 2 outputs has a \(2\times3\) weight matrix \(W\), and each output is one row of \(W\) combined with the input. Real layers are huge — one example in Build a Large Language Model from Scratch is \(2048\times2048 = 4{,}194{,}304\) numbers, more than four million.

4b · Worked example 3

Order matters: stretch and rotate. The stretch \(S\) has columns \((2,0),(0,1)\) (twice as wide); the rotation \(R\) has columns \((0,1),(-1,0)\) (a quarter turn left):

$$ S=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad R=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix} $$

Stretch-then-rotate \((RS)\) is not rotate-then-stretch \((SR)\):

$$ RS=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\2&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad SR=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-2\\1&0\end{bmatrix} $$

Follow \(e_1=(1,0)\): under \(RS\) it lands at \((0,2)\) (the first column of \(RS\)); under \(SR\) it lands at \((0,1)\). So \(RS\neq SR\).

5 · Common mistakes

Assuming AB = BA. With ordinary numbers, a·b = b·a. With matrices, in general AB ≠ BA, as the stretch and rotation show. Sometimes only one order is even allowed: a 2×3 times a 3×4 works (result 2×4), but 3×4 times 2×3 does not.

Transposing by "pouring" numbers. For rows (1, 2, 3) and (4, 5, 6), pouring in reading order gives (1, 2), (3, 4), (5, 6). That is wrong. The real transpose has rows (1, 4), (2, 5), (3, 6): entry (i, j) comes from entry (j, i).

Mixing up row and column in an address. a₂₁ means row 2, column 1. Rows always come first.

6 · Where it comes from & where ML uses it

A matrix times a vector is a linear combination of its columns, and matrix multiplication is one such machine after another. So everything here is built from adding and scaling: axiom A, linearity. Grids stay straight, parallel and evenly spaced, and the origin stays fixed. Such machines are linear maps (video 04).

  • A batch of data is a matrix with one row per example.
  • Every layer stores its weights in a matrix W and computes W x.
  • Scoring many examples at once is one product, X w.
  • Stacking layers is matrix multiplication, so the order of the matrices matters.
  • The transpose appears everywhere, for example to turn a row of weights into a column.

7 · Try it yourself

Q1. Multiply the matrix with rows (1, 2) and (0, 1) by the vector (3, 1).

Show the answer

Row view: 1·3 + 2·1 = 5 and 0·3 + 1·1 = 1, so (5, 1). Column view: 3·(1, 0) + 1·(2, 1) = (3, 0) + (2, 1) = (5, 1). Same answer.

Q2. A is 2×3 and B is 3×4. Is AB allowed? What is its shape? Is BA allowed?

Show the answer

AB: the inner numbers are 3 and 3, so it is allowed, and the result is 2×4. BA: B is 3×4 and A is 2×3, so the inner numbers are 4 and 2. They do not match, so BA is not allowed.

Q3 (new). Where does the rotation R (columns (0, 1) and (−1, 0)) send the vector (1, 2)?

Show the answer

1·(0, 1) + 2·(−1, 0) = (0, 1) + (−2, 0) = (−2, 1).

In summary
  • A matrix is an m×n grid of numbers; as a table, each row is a data point.
  • As a machine, A x is a linear combination of the columns, and the columns show where e₁ and e₂ land.
  • Matrix multiplication is one machine after another: m×n times n×p gives m×p, and the order matters.
  • The transpose swaps rows and columns; the identity matrix changes nothing.
03

Basis and rank

The rank counts how many truly independent directions a set of vectors (or the columns of a matrix) contains, once you remove every redundant one.

Needs: videos 01 and 02 · Axiom: A — linearity

1 · The big idea

Imagine a data table with one hundred columns. It looks like one hundred different directions. But some columns may only repeat others. A length in meters and the same length in centimeters say the same thing twice. A vector that brings no new direction is called redundant.

Redundancy can hide. Take a = (2, 1), b = (1, 2) and c = (3, 3). No two of them are parallel. But walk along a, then along b, and you arrive at (3, 3). So c = a + b. Anything you build with c, you can already build with a and b. Three vectors, but only two real directions.

This video gives us a precise way to count real directions. A set with no redundant vector is linearly independent. A smallest set of building blocks that still reaches the whole space is a basis. The number of vectors in a basis is the dimension. For a matrix, the number of independent columns is the rank. In ML, rank tells us when features are redundant, and it is the key idea behind LoRA, a cheap way to fine-tune large language models.

2 · The formal definition

A set of vectors is linearly independent if no vector in it is a linear combination of the others. The equivalent test, which is easier to check:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i\, x_i = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \dots = \lambda_k = 0 $$
\(\lambda_i\)
"lambda i" — lambda is a Greek letter. Here λᵢ is a weight, a scalar.
\(x_i\)
"x i" — the i-th vector of the set.
\(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\)
"the sum from i equals 1 to k" — add up all k weighted vectors.
\(= 0\)
"equals the zero vector".
\(\Longrightarrow\)
"implies" — if the left side is true, then the right side must be true.

In words: the only way to combine the vectors into zero is with all-zero weights. If some other weights also give zero, the set is linearly dependent.

A basis is a linearly independent set that spans the whole space. In a basis, every point p has exactly one set of coordinates:

$$ p = c_1\, b_1 + c_2\, b_2 $$
\(b_1, b_2\)
"b one", "b two" — the basis vectors.
\(c_1, c_2\)
"c one", "c two" — the coordinates of p in this basis: the weights that build p.

Every basis of a space has the same number of vectors: the dimension. The plane has dimension 2. Finally, for a matrix A:

$$ \operatorname{rank}(A) = \text{number of independent columns} = \text{number of independent rows} $$
\(\operatorname{rank}(A)\)
"the rank of A" — also the dimension of the column space, the set of all combinations of the columns.

The rank can never be larger than the number of rows or the number of columns. When it is as large as possible, the matrix has full rank.

3 · Worked example 1

Test independence, then change basis — in matrix form. The trick: put the vectors as the columns of a matrix. Then independence and change of basis each become one matrix operation.

Step 1. Are \(a=(2,1)\), \(b=(1,2)\), \(c=(3,3)\) independent? Stack them as columns and ask whether \(M\lambda=0\) has any non-zero \(\lambda\):

$$ M\lambda=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&3\\1&2&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2+1-3\\1+2-3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix} $$

A non-zero \(\lambda=(1,1,-1)\) gives \(0\), so the columns are dependent. (Clear in matrix form: a \(2\times3\) matrix has rank ≤ 2, so 3 columns can never all be independent.)

Step 2. Are \(a\) and \(b\) alone independent? For a square block, independence just means a non-zero determinant:

$$ A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \det(A)=2\cdot2-1\cdot1=3\neq0\;\Rightarrow\;\text{independent.} $$

The whole "suppose \(\lambda_1\dots\lambda_2\dots\)" algebra collapses to one determinant.

Step 3. Describe \(p=(1,3)\) in the basis \(b_1=(1,1)\), \(b_2=(-1,1)\). Put the new basis as the columns of \(B\); the coordinates \(c\) are the solution of \(Bc=p\):

$$ Bc=p:\qquad \begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\c_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\3\end{bmatrix} $$

That one matrix equation is the two equations \(c_1-c_2=1\) and \(c_1+c_2=3\). Solve by inverting \(B\):

$$ \det(B)=2,\quad B^{-1}=\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix},\quad c=B^{-1}p=\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\3\end{bmatrix}=\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}4\\2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix} $$

Same point, two descriptions. \(p\) is \((1,3)\) seen through the standard axes \(e_1,e_2\), and \((2,1)\) seen through the axes \(b_1,b_2\). The matrix \(B\) turns \(b\)-coordinates into standard coordinates (\(Bc=p\)); its inverse \(B^{-1}\) turns standard coordinates into \(b\)-coordinates (\(c=B^{-1}p\)). A matrix is the translator between two viewpoints.

4 · Worked example 2

Find the rank of three matrices, then of a data table.

Matrix 1 has columns (1, 2, 0), (−1, −2, 0), (3, 6, 0). Column 2 is −1 times column 1. Column 3 is 3 times column 1. One independent column, so rank 1. The column space is a line.

Matrix 2 has columns (1, 0, 1), (0, 1, 1), (1, 1, 2). The first two are not parallel. But column 3 = column 1 + column 2: (1+0, 0+1, 1+1) = (1, 1, 2). So rank 2. The column space is a flat plane through the origin.

The 3×3 identity matrix has three independent columns, so rank 3: full rank.

ML table. Three objects: length in meters (1.2, 0.8, 2.0), length in centimeters (120, 80, 200), weight in kg (5, 3, 9). The cm column is 100 times the m column: 100·1.2 = 120, 100·0.8 = 80, 100·2.0 = 200. Three columns, but rank 2. The cm feature adds no new information.

5 · Common mistakes

Confusing dimension with the number of entries. The span of (0, 1) is a line. Its vectors have two entries, but the line has dimension 1. A flat plane through the origin in 3D space has dimension 2.

Checking only pairs. a, b, c above have no parallel pair, yet they are dependent. Always test the whole set. Also: three vectors in the plane are always dependent, because the plane has dimension 2.

Forgetting the zero vector. Any set that contains the zero vector is dependent, because 1 times the zero vector is already zero.

6 · Where it comes from & where ML uses it

Independence, basis, dimension and rank all ask one question about linear combinations: which points can we reach by adding and scaling? So the whole video rests on axiom A, linearity. In practice, a computer counts the rank for you.

  • Detecting redundant features, like the same length in meters and in centimeters.
  • LoRA (low-rank adaptation) lets each weight matrix change only along a few directions, for example 16 out of 768, so there are far fewer numbers to train.
  • Coordinates in a new basis: the same data point can be described with different building blocks.
  • The column space of a weight matrix is the set of all outputs a layer can produce.

04

Linear mapping

A linear mapping is a function that respects adding and scaling, and every such map is a matrix whose columns are where the basis vectors land.

Needs: videos 01, 02 and 03 · Axiom: A — linearity

1 · The big idea

Every layer of a neural network starts with the same step. A vector goes in, and a new vector comes out. This step respects adding and scaling. A function like this is called a linear mapping (or linear map). If you understand linear maps, you know exactly what a layer can do, and also what it can never do on its own.

"Respects adding and scaling" means this: you can combine first and then apply the map, or apply the map first and then combine. The result is the same. In a picture, a linear map moves the whole plane, but grid lines stay straight, parallel lines stay parallel, the spacing stays even, and the origin stays where it is. Stretches, rotations, shears and projections are all linear. A shear slides each horizontal line sideways, more and more the higher it is, like pushing the top of a deck of cards. A projection onto a line drops every point straight down onto that line.

The best news: every linear map is a matrix, and you only need two arrows to find it. Its columns are where e₁ = (1, 0) and e₂ = (0, 1) land. That is where the rotation matrix of video 02 came from.

2 · The formal definition

$$ f(a\,u + b\,v) = a\,f(u) + b\,f(v) \quad \text{for all vectors } u, v \text{ and all scalars } a, b $$
\(f\)
"f" — the function. It takes a vector in and gives a vector out.
\(u, v\)
"u", "v" — any two input vectors.
\(a, b\)
"a", "b" — any two scalars, the weights.
\(f(a u + b v)\)
"f of a u plus b v" — combine first, then apply f.
\(a f(u) + b f(v)\)
"a f of u plus b f of v" — apply f first, then combine the outputs with the same weights.

This is the same as two simpler rules: f(u + v) = f(u) + f(v), and f(c u) = c f(u). One consequence: with c = 0 we get f(0) = 0. A linear map always sends zero to zero. Next, the matrix of the map:

$$ A = \big[\, f(e_1) \;\; f(e_2) \,\big], \qquad f(x) = x_1 f(e_1) + x_2 f(e_2) = A\,x $$
\(e_1, e_2\)
"e one", "e two" — the standard basis vectors (1, 0) and (0, 1).
\(f(e_1)\)
"f of e one" — where e₁ lands. It is the first column of A.
\(x_1, x_2\)
"x one", "x two" — the components of x, because x = x₁e₁ + x₂e₂.

Two more words. The kernel (or null space) is the set of all inputs that the map sends to zero. The image (or range) is the set of all outputs. They balance:

$$ \dim(\ker f) + \dim(\operatorname{im} f) = \dim(\text{input space}) $$
\(\dim\)
"the dimension of" — the number of vectors in a basis (video 03).
\(\ker f\)
"the kernel of f" — what the map destroys.
\(\operatorname{im} f\)
"the image of f" — what the map keeps. Its dimension is the rank of the matrix.

3 · Worked example 1

Build a matrix from two arrows, then test functions that are not linear.

Step 1. A linear map sends \(e_1\) to \((2,1)\) and \(e_2\) to \((-1,1)\). The columns of \(A\) are exactly those images:

$$ A=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix} $$

Step 2. Where does \((3,2)\) go? Apply the matrix — a combination of its columns:

$$ A\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix}=3\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}+2\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}4\\5\end{bmatrix} $$

Step 3. Test the shift f(x) = x + 1. f(0) = 1, not 0, so it is not linear. Also f(2 + 3) = f(5) = 6, but f(2) + f(3) = 3 + 4 = 7. A shift like this is an affine map: a linear map followed by a move.

Step 4. Test the square f(x) = x². f(1 + 1) = f(2) = 4, but f(1) + f(1) = 1 + 1 = 2. Scaling fails too: f(2·3) = 36, but 2·f(3) = 2·9 = 18. Not linear.

4 · Worked example 2

Compose a shear and a projection; find the kernel and image.

The shear \(S\) and the projection \(P\) onto the horizontal axis are:

$$ S=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad P=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix} $$

Shear first, then project — compose the two by multiplying \(PS\):

$$ PS=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix} $$

Check with \((1,2)\) — one matrix does both steps:

$$ PS\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\0\end{bmatrix} $$

Kernel of P: every point on the vertical axis, like (0, 5), drops to (0, 0). So the kernel is the vertical axis, dimension 1. Image of P: the horizontal axis, dimension 1, which is the rank of P. Balance: 1 + 1 = 2, the dimension of the plane.

4b · Worked example 3

An ML example: two linear layers collapse into one. Layer 1 maps 2 numbers to 3, layer 2 maps 3 numbers to 2:

$$ W_1=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad W_2=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\\2&1&0\end{bmatrix} $$

Composing the layers is one matrix product:

$$ W_2W_1=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\\2&1&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&2\\2&5\end{bmatrix} $$

Test with \(y=(1,2)\) — layer by layer equals the single matrix:

$$ W_1y=\begin{bmatrix}5\\2\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad W_2(W_1y)=\begin{bmatrix}6\\12\end{bmatrix}=(W_2W_1)y $$

Two linear layers are just one matrix.

5 · Common mistakes

Calling every straight line "linear". The graph of x + 1 is straight, but it misses the origin, so it is affine, not linear. The fastest test is f(0) = 0.

Thinking a deep stack of linear layers is more powerful. W₂(W₁x) = (W₂W₁)x. Ten linear layers can only do what one matrix can do. Networks need a nonlinear activation between layers (video 16).

Thinking a projection is "too extreme" to be linear. It squashes the plane onto a line, but lines stay straight and evenly spaced, the origin stays fixed, and the rule holds.

6 · Where it comes from & where ML uses it

The definition of a linear map is exactly axiom A: it respects adding and scaling. The matrix, composition, kernel and image all follow from that one rule.

  • A PyTorch linear layer computes W x + b. W x is linear; adding the bias vector b moves the origin, so the whole layer is affine.
  • Stacking layers is composing maps, so the combined matrix is the product of the matrices.
  • GPT-2 has a block that expands each vector of 768 numbers to 3072 and then brings it back. Without a nonlinearity in between, the two maps would collapse into one.
  • The kernel tells you which input directions a layer ignores completely.

05

Inner product

The inner product turns two vectors into one number that says how much they point the same way, and it is the source of all angles and lengths.

Needs: video 01 (vectors) and video 02 (matrices, the transpose) · Axiom: B — the inner product

1 · The big idea

How does a language model know that "cat" is closer to "dog" than to "car"? Every word is a vector. To compare two vectors, the model computes one single number. This number says how much the two vectors point the same way. That number is the inner product, and the most common inner product is the dot product.

The recipe is short. Multiply the matching components, first with first, second with second, and so on. Then add all the products. The picture behind the recipe is a shadow. Shine a light from above onto the line of one vector. The other vector casts a shadow on that line. This shadow is called a projection. The dot product is the length of one vector times the length of the shadow of the other.

Why does this matter in ML? In attention, each word asks every other word: "how related are we?" The answer is a dot product, called an attention score. Similarity search uses it to find documents like a query. And a single neuron computes \(w \cdot x\), a weighted sum, which is also a dot product. The video calls the inner product "the engine inside attention."

2 · The formal definition

The dot product of two vectors with the same number of components:

$$ x \cdot y \;=\; x^\top y \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i\, y_i $$
\(x \cdot y\)
"x dot y" — the dot product of the vectors x and y. The result is one number, not a vector.
\(x^\top y\)
"x transpose y" — the same thing in matrix language: a row times a column gives a 1-by-1 matrix, just a number.
\(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\)
"the sum, for i from 1 to n" — add up the terms that follow, one for each component.
\(x_i\, y_i\)
"x sub i times y sub i" — the i-th component of x multiplied by the i-th component of y.

The geometric meaning gives a famous formula:

$$ x \cdot y \;=\; \|x\|\,\|y\|\,\cos\theta $$
\(\|x\|\)
"the length of x" — the usual length of the arrow. Video 06 makes this exact.
\(\theta\)
"theta" — the angle between the two arrows.
\(\cos\theta\)
"cosine of theta" — 1 when the angle is 0, 0 at a right angle, −1 when the arrows point in opposite directions.

If we divide by the two lengths, we keep only the direction. This is cosine similarity:

$$ \cos\theta \;=\; \frac{x \cdot y}{\|x\|\,\|y\|} \qquad (\text{always between } -1 \text{ and } 1) $$
\(\frac{x \cdot y}{\|x\|\,\|y\|}\)
"x dot y over the length of x times the length of y" — the dot product with the sizes removed. 1 = same direction, 0 = orthogonal, −1 = opposite.

Mathematicians also write a general inner product with angle brackets. Any rule that pairs two vectors into one number is an inner product if it follows three rules:

$$ \langle x, y\rangle = \langle y, x\rangle, \qquad \langle a x + b z,\, y\rangle = a\langle x, y\rangle + b\langle z, y\rangle, \qquad \langle x, x\rangle > 0 \text{ for } x \neq 0 $$
\(\langle x, y\rangle\)
"the inner product of x and y" — the general version; the dot product is one example.
\(\langle x, y\rangle = \langle y, x\rangle\)
Rule 1, symmetric: the order does not matter.
\(\langle a x + b z, y\rangle\)
Rule 2, linear in each input: scale or add inside one slot, and the result scales or adds the same way. (Here a and b are numbers, and z is another vector.)
\(\langle x, x\rangle > 0\)
Rule 3, positive: a vector paired with itself gives a positive number, unless the vector is zero.

A vector space only knows how to add and scale. It has no lengths and no angles. The inner product adds them. That is why the series calls it axiom B: the source of all geometry.

3 · Worked example 1

Compute a dot product, then an angle.

Let \(x = (1, 2, 3)\) and \(y = (4, -5, 6)\).

The dot product is a row vector times a column vector:

$$ x^\top y=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}4\\-5\\6\end{bmatrix}=1\cdot4+2\cdot(-5)+3\cdot6=4-10+18=12 $$

Now find the angle between \(a = (1, 1)\) and \(b = (1, 2)\).

  1. Dot product: \(1 \times 1 + 1 \times 2 = 3\).
  2. Lengths by Pythagoras: \(\|a\| = \sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt{2}\), and \(\|b\| = \sqrt{1 + 4} = \sqrt{5}\).
  3. Cosine: \(\cos\theta = \dfrac{3}{\sqrt{2}\,\sqrt{5}} = \dfrac{3}{\sqrt{10}} \approx \dfrac{3}{3.1623} \approx 0.9487\).
  4. Angle: \(\theta \approx 18.4^\circ\). The arrows point almost the same way, and we needed no protractor.

4 · Worked example 2 (and a third, from attention)

Toy word vectors: is "cat" closer to "dog" or to "car"? These are small made-up numbers, not real embeddings.

5 · Common mistakes

Expecting a vector as the answer. The dot product multiplies and then adds. The result is one number. If you stop before adding, you have the wrong object.

Reading a zero dot product as "no answer". Zero has a meaning: the vectors are orthogonal, at a right angle. For example, \((1,1)\cdot(-1,1) = -1 + 1 = 0\). Also, the zero vector is orthogonal to every vector.

Comparing raw dot products when you care only about direction. A long arrow gives a big dot product even if its direction is a little off. Divide by the lengths (cosine similarity) to compare directions fairly.

6 · Where it comes from & where ML uses it

The dot product comes from the shadow picture: one length times the projection of the other vector. It is also hidden inside matrix multiplication. In video 02, the row view of a matrix times a vector was exactly this: multiply matching entries and add. Every entry of a matrix product is a dot product. The general inner product is axiom B, and video 06 gets lengths from it.

  • Attention scores in transformers: a query vector dotted with each key vector.
  • Similarity search: compare a query vector with many stored vectors and keep those with the largest cosine similarity.
  • A single neuron: \(w \cdot x\), the weights dotted with the input.
  • Orthogonal features: features with zero dot product carry separate information, with no overlap.

Show the answer

Norm

A norm measures the size of a vector as one number, and the norm of a difference is a distance.

Needs: video 01 (vectors) and video 05 (inner product) · Axiom: B — the inner product (the L2 norm comes from it)

1 · The big idea

How big is an error? How far apart are two words inside a language model? Is one training step too large? Each answer is one number that measures the size of a vector. That number is called the norm. A norm is never negative.

Start with an arrow that goes 3 steps right and 4 steps up. The two steps and the arrow make a right triangle. Pythagoras says the arrow has length 5. But a taxi in a city with a grid of streets cannot drive through the buildings. It drives 3 blocks right and 4 blocks up: 7 blocks. And if you only care about the biggest step, the answer is 4. So one vector can have three sizes: 5, 7 and 4. All three are real norms. They measure different things.

In ML, norms are everywhere. They keep weights small (regularization). They cap huge gradients (gradient clipping). They find the closest data point (nearest neighbour search). And they turn any vector into a pure direction (a unit vector).

2 · The formal definition

The standard norm is the square root of the dot product of a vector with itself:

$$ \|x\|_2 \;=\; \sqrt{x \cdot x} \;=\; \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^2} $$
\(\|x\|_2\)
"the L2 norm of x" (also "the norm of x" or "the Euclidean norm") — the straight-line length of the arrow. The double bars mean "norm".
\(x \cdot x\)
"x dot x" — the dot product of x with itself, from video 05.
\(\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^2\)
"the sum, for i from 1 to n, of x sub i squared" — square each component and add them all.
\(\sqrt{\;}\)
"the square root of" — undoes the squaring, so the answer is a length.

Two other common norms:

$$ \|x\|_1 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i| \qquad\qquad \|x\|_\infty = \max_i |x_i| $$
\(\|x\|_1\)
"the L1 norm of x" (the Manhattan norm) — the taxi distance: add the absolute values.
\(|x_i|\)
"the absolute value of x sub i" — the number without its minus sign, so −3 counts as 3.
\(\|x\|_\infty\)
"the L-infinity norm of x" (the max norm) — keep only the largest absolute value.
\(\max_i\)
"the maximum over i" — the biggest of all the values.

Every norm must follow three rules:

$$ \|x\| \ge 0 \text{ (and } = 0 \text{ only for } x = 0), \qquad \|c\,x\| = |c|\,\|x\|, \qquad \|u + v\| \le \|u\| + \|v\| $$
\(\|x\| \ge 0\)
Rule 1 — the norm is never negative. It is zero only for the zero vector.
\(\|c\,x\| = |c|\,\|x\|\)
Rule 2 — multiply a vector by a number c, and the norm is multiplied by the absolute value of c.
\(\|u+v\| \le \|u\|+\|v\|\)
Rule 3, the triangle inequality — the straight route is never longer than the detour.

From a norm we get a unit vector and a distance:

$$ \hat{x} = \frac{x}{\|x\|} \qquad\qquad d(x, y) = \|x - y\| $$
\(\hat{x}\)
"x hat" — the unit vector: x divided by its own norm. Its norm is exactly 1. This step is called normalizing.
\(d(x, y)\)
"the distance between x and y" — the norm of the difference x − y. A distance rule like this is called a metric.

3 · Worked example 1

Three sizes of one vector, then a vector in 3D.

Let \(x = (3, 4)\).

  1. L2 (the usual length) is \(\sqrt{x^\top x}\): \(\begin{bmatrix}3&4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3\\4\end{bmatrix}=9+16=25\), and \(\sqrt{25}=5\).
  2. L1: \(|3| + |4| = 3 + 4 = 7\).
  3. L-infinity: \(\max(3, 4) = 4\).

Now \(x = (2, 3, 6)\): squares \(4, 9, 36\); sum \(49\); \(\sqrt{49} = 7\). With a million components, a computer does exactly the same steps.

Normalize \((3, 4)\): divide each component by its norm 5. You get \((0.6, 0.8)\). Check: \(0.6^2 + 0.8^2 = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1\), and \(\sqrt{1} = 1\). The direction stays. Only the length changes.

Triangle inequality: \(u = (3, 4)\), \(v = (4, -3)\). Each has norm 5, so the detour is 10. Their sum is \((7, 1)\), with norm \(\sqrt{49 + 1} = \sqrt{50} \approx 7.07\). And \(7.07 \le 10\).

4 · Worked example 2

Two ML jobs: clip a gradient, and find a nearest neighbour.

Gradient clipping. A toy gradient is \(g = (6, 8)\). Its norm is \(\sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10\). The maximum allowed norm is 5.

  1. The scale factor is \(5 / 10 = 0.5\).
  2. New gradient: \(0.5 \times (6, 8) = (3, 4)\).
  3. Its norm is \(\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5\). The direction is kept. Only the length is capped.

Nearest neighbour. The query point is \(q = (2, 2)\). The toy data points are A \(= (1, 5)\), B \(= (4, 3)\), C \(= (0, 0)\).

  1. A − q \(= (-1, 3)\): distance \(\sqrt{1 + 9} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16\).
  2. B − q \(= (2, 1)\): distance \(\sqrt{4 + 1} = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.24\).
  3. C − q \(= (-2, -2)\): distance \(\sqrt{4 + 4} = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.83\).

The smallest distance is 2.24, so B is the nearest neighbour.

5 · Common mistakes

Trying to normalize the zero vector. Its norm is 0, and we cannot divide by 0. The zero vector has no direction, so it cannot be normalized.

Forgetting the absolute value when you scale. \(-2 \times (3, 4) = (-6, -8)\). The arrow flips, but the norm is \(|{-2}| \times 5 = 10\), not \(-10\). A norm is never negative.

Expecting angles from every norm. Only the L2 norm comes from an inner product. The L1 and L-infinity norms are real norms, but no inner product gives them (a test called the parallelogram law proves this). They give sizes, but no angles.

6 · Where it comes from & where ML uses it

The L2 norm comes from the inner product, axiom B: it is the "length" part of the geometry that the inner product gives a vector space. In two dimensions it is exactly Pythagoras. Distance then comes from length: the norm of the difference. Notice one more thing. Distance and the inner product move in opposite directions. Two very similar vectors have a large inner product but a small distance.

  • L2 regularization (ridge regression): add \(\lambda \|w\|_2^2\) to the loss, so the model prefers small weights and fits less noise.
  • L1 regularization (lasso): the L1 penalty pushes many weights to exactly zero. This is called sparsity. The sharp corners of the L1 "diamond" sit on the axes, where one weight is zero.
  • Gradient clipping: if the gradient's L2 norm is above a maximum, scale it down. A standard trick when training large language models.
  • Nearest neighbour search: find the stored vectors with the smallest distance to a query, even over millions of word vectors.
  • Cosine similarity (video 05): the dot product of two normalized vectors.

Derivative

The derivative is the slope of a curve at one point, and it tells a model how the error changes when you nudge one weight.

Needs: only school algebra (functions, graphs, powers like x²) · Axiom: C — local linearity

1 · The big idea

A neural network learns by changing its weights a tiny bit at a time. Before each change, it asks one question for every weight: "If I nudge this weight a little, how much does the error change, and in which direction?" The number that answers this question is the derivative.

A straight line has one slope everywhere: rise divided by run. A curve like \(f(x) = x^2\) is different. Near 0 it is flat; far to the right it is steep. So what is its slope at the single point \(x = 3\)? With only one point, rise over run makes no sense yet. The trick: take a second point a small step \(h\) away and draw the line through both points. Its slope is the average slope. Now shrink \(h\). The line turns and settles on one special line that just touches the curve. This is the tangent line, and its slope is the slope of the curve right there.

The key idea of the whole calculus part of the series: zoom in on a smooth curve, and it looks like a straight line. So near one point you can replace the curve with its tangent. The series calls this axiom C: local linearity. It is the bridge between calculus and linear algebra.

2 · The formal definition

$$ f'(x) \;=\; \frac{df}{dx} \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} $$
\(f'(x)\)
"f prime of x" — the derivative of f at the point x: the slope there.
\(\frac{df}{dx}\)
"d f over d x" — another way to write the same derivative: change in output over change in input.
\(\lim_{h \to 0}\)
"the limit as h goes to zero" — the value that the expression gets closer and closer to as h shrinks. We never put h = 0 itself.
\(f(x+h) - f(x)\)
"f of x plus h, minus f of x" — the rise: how much the output changes.
\(h\)
"h" — the run: a small step in the input.

Local linearity (axiom C) says that, for a small step, the curve is almost its tangent line:

$$ f(x + h) \;\approx\; f(x) + f'(x)\, h $$
\(\approx\)
"is approximately" — close, but not exactly equal. The error gets smaller as h gets smaller.
\(f'(x)\,h\)
"f prime of x times h" — the slope times the step. Double the step, and this change doubles: a linear mapping of the step.

Three quick rules do most of the work:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}x^n = n\,x^{n-1}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\big(c\,f\big) = c\,f', \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\big(f + g\big) = f' + g' $$
\(n\,x^{n-1}\)
The power rule: "the derivative of x to the n is n times x to the n minus one". So \(x^2 \to 2x\) and \(x^3 \to 3x^2\).
\(c\,f'\)
The constant rule: a constant factor c stays in front.
\(f' + g'\)
The sum rule: take each piece on its own and add.

3 · Worked example 1

The slope of \(x^2\) at \(x = 3\), as a limit.

  1. \(h = 1\): \(f(4) - f(3) = 16 - 9 = 7\); divide by 1: 7.
  2. \(h = 0.1\): \(3.1^2 = 9.61\); \(9.61 - 9 = 0.61\); divide by 0.1: 6.1.
  3. \(h = 0.01\): \(3.01^2 = 9.0601\); \(9.0601 - 9 = 0.0601\); divide by 0.01: 6.01.
  4. The numbers 7, 6.1, 6.01 walk toward 6. So \(f'(3) = 6\).

Why 6? Expand: \((x + h)^2 = x^2 + 2xh + h^2\). Subtract \(x^2\): \(2xh + h^2\). Divide by \(h\): \(2x + h\). As \(h \to 0\), this becomes \(2x\). At \(x = 3\), \(2x = 6\).

Local linear check. Guess \(3.1^2\): \(9 + 6 \times 0.1 = 9.6\). The true value is 9.61. The guess is wrong by only 0.01.

Another curve. For \(x^3\) at \(x = 2\), the power rule gives \(3 \times 2^2 = 12\). Guess \(2.1^3\): \(8 + 12 \times 0.1 = 9.2\). The true value is 9.261. Again very close.

4 · Worked example 2

Use the rules, then a toy ML loss.

\(f(x) = 3x^2 + 5x\). The derivative of \(x^2\) is \(2x\), so \(3x^2\) gives \(6x\). The derivative of \(5x\) is 5. So \(f'(x) = 6x + 5\), and at \(x = 1\) the slope is \(6 + 5 = 11\).

Now a loss (an error score: smaller is better; video 15) for a toy model with one weight \(w\):

  1. \(L(w) = (2w - 6)^2 = 4w^2 - 24w + 36\).
  2. Rules: \(L'(w) = 8w - 24\).
  3. At \(w = 1\): \(L'(1) = 8 - 24 = -16\).
  4. Nudge \(w\) up by 0.01. Predicted change: \(-16 \times 0.01 = -0.16\).
  5. True change: \(L(1) = (-4)^2 = 16\) and \(L(1.01) = (-3.98)^2 = 15.8404\), so the change is \(-0.1596\).

The sign tells the direction: the slope is negative, so increasing \(w\) lowers the loss. The size tells how fast. At \(w = 3\), \(L'(3) = 0\): the bottom, the best weight.

5 · Common mistakes

Putting h = 0 directly. Then you get \(0/0\), which means nothing. A limit only asks where the ratio is heading as h shrinks.

Thinking a zero derivative always means a minimum or maximum. At a minimum (the bottom of \((x-2)^2 + 1\), at \(x = 2\)) or a maximum, the tangent is flat. But for \(x^3\), the slope at 0 is 0, and the curve keeps going up on both sides. A zero slope only tells you where to look.

Trusting the linear guess for big steps. \(f(x+h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)h\) is good only close to x. For smaller steps, the guess gets better.

6 · Where it comes from & where ML uses it

The derivative comes from the average slope (rise over run) between two points, with the run shrinking toward zero. Its deepest meaning is local linearity: near one point, the change in output is about one fixed number (the slope) times the step. One more rule, for a function inside another function, gets its own video: the chain rule, video 09.

  • Training direction: the sign of \(L'(w)\) says whether to increase or decrease a weight.
  • Finding the best weight: at the bottom of the loss, the derivative is zero.
  • Predicting a change: \(L(w + h) \approx L(w) + L'(w)h\) tells how much one small update helps.
  • Gradients (video 08): a real model takes one derivative per weight and bundles them into a vector.
  • Checking code: the ratio \((f(x+h) - f(x))/h\) with a tiny h is a quick numeric test of a derivative.

  • The power, constant and sum rules make derivatives quick. At a minimum or maximum, the derivative is zero (but zero does not guarantee one).
  • In ML, the derivative says how the loss changes when you nudge one weight: its sign gives the direction, its size the speed.
08

Gradient

The gradient bundles one slope per input into a vector that points straight uphill, so minus the gradient points the way down.

Needs: video 05 (inner product), video 06 (norm) and video 07 (derivative) · Axiom: C — local linearity (written with the inner product)

1 · The big idea

In video 07, one weight had one slope. But a real model has millions of weights, and its error depends on all of them at the same time. So we need one slope for each weight. Bundle all those slopes into one vector, and you get the gradient. It is the arrow that tells a model which way is uphill.

Picture a function of two inputs, like \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2\), as a landscape. Each point of the flat x-y plane gets a height. This one is a bowl, with its lowest point at the origin. Hikers read such a landscape with a contour map: a contour line joins all the points that have the same height. Where the lines are close together, the ground is steep. Where they are far apart, the ground is gentle.

At one point, how steep is the ground? It depends on the direction you walk. Start with the two simplest directions: walk only along x (keep y frozen), or only along y (keep x frozen). Each of these slopes is a partial derivative. The gradient is the vector of all partial derivatives. In ML, the landscape is the loss, and the small GPT-2 model has about 124 million weights, so its gradient has about 124 million entries.

2 · The formal definition

$$ \nabla f(x, y) \;=\; \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\; \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \right) $$
\(\nabla f\)
"nabla f" or "the gradient of f" — the upside-down triangle is called nabla. The result is a vector, an arrow on the map.
\(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\)
"partial f, partial x" — the slope of f when only x moves. Treat every other input as a constant number and use the normal rules from video 07. The curly d means "partial".
\(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\)
"partial f, partial y" — the slope of f when only y moves and x is frozen.

Local linearity (axiom C) with many inputs: the step \(h\) is now a vector, and "slope times step" becomes a dot product:

$$ f(\mathbf{x} + h) \;\approx\; f(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) \cdot h $$
\(\mathbf{x}\)
"x" (bold) — the current point, a vector of all the inputs.
\(h\)
"h" — a small step, also a vector.
\(\nabla f \cdot h\)
"the gradient dot h" — multiply each partial slope by the step in that direction, and add the results (video 05).

Why does the gradient point uphill? Take a step of length 1 in a direction \(u\). By the cosine formula from video 05:

$$ \nabla f \cdot u \;=\; \|\nabla f\|\,\|u\|\cos\theta \;=\; \|\nabla f\|\cos\theta $$
\(u\)
"u" — a unit vector (norm 1, video 06): a pure direction to walk in.
\(\|\nabla f\|\)
"the norm of the gradient" — how steep the steepest direction is.
\(\theta\)
"theta" — the angle between u and the gradient. At 0° the change is largest (uphill fastest); at 90° it is zero (along a contour line); at 180° it is most negative (downhill fastest).

3 · Worked example 1

Partial derivatives and the gradient of \(f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y\) at \((1, 2)\).

  1. Freeze \(y = 2\): \(f = x^2 + 6\). Its derivative is \(2x\). At \(x = 1\): 2.
  2. Freeze \(x = 1\): \(f = 1 + 3y\). Its derivative is 3, everywhere.
  3. Stack the two partials into the gradient column vector: \(\nabla f(1,2)=\begin{bmatrix}2\\3\end{bmatrix}\).

Linear check. \(f(1, 2) = 1 + 6 = 7\). Step by \(h = (0.1, 0.1)\):

  1. \(\nabla f^\top h=\begin{bmatrix}2&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0.1\\0.1\end{bmatrix}=0.2+0.3=0.5\).
  2. Guess: \(7 + 0.5 = 7.5\).
  3. True: \(f(1.1, 2.1) = 1.21 + 6.3 = 7.51\). Very close.

4 · Worked example 2

The bowl \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2\) at \((3, 4)\), and one step downhill.

  1. Height: \(f(3, 4) = 9 + 16 = 25\).
  2. Partials: \(\partial f/\partial x = 2x\), \(\partial f/\partial y = 2y\). So \(\nabla f(3,4)=\begin{bmatrix}6\\8\end{bmatrix}\).
  3. Steepness: \(\|\nabla f\| = \sqrt{36 + 64} = 10\). Walking along the gradient, the height rises at rate 10. At 45° it rises at \(10 \cos 45^\circ \approx 7.07\); at 90°, 0; at 180°, −10.
  4. Linear check with \(h = (0.1, -0.1)\): \(\nabla f \cdot h = 0.6 - 0.8 = -0.2\). Guess \(24.8\); true \(3.1^2 + 3.9^2 = 9.61 + 15.21 = 24.82\).

One toy training step. Step against the gradient, with a step size of 0.1:

  1. New point \(x-\eta\,\nabla f\): \(\begin{bmatrix}3\\4\end{bmatrix}-0.1\begin{bmatrix}6\\8\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2.4\\3.2\end{bmatrix}\).
  2. New height: \(2.4^2 + 3.2^2 = 5.76 + 10.24 = 16\).
  3. The height drops from 25 to 16 in one step.

5 · Common mistakes

Forgetting to freeze the other inputs. In \(\partial f/\partial x\), every other input is a constant. For \(3x + y^2\), the partial with respect to x is just 3; \(y^2\) is a constant there, so its derivative is 0.

Walking with the gradient to reduce the loss. The gradient points steepest uphill. To go down, step against it: use minus the gradient. This is the seed of gradient descent.

Worrying about row versus arrow. Some books, including Mathematics for Machine Learning, write the gradient as a row of numbers. Here it is drawn as an arrow. It is the same information. In ML code, the gradient usually has exactly the same shape as the weights.

6 · Where it comes from & where ML uses it

The gradient comes from video 07's derivative, taken once per input with the other inputs frozen. Local linearity then says the change in height is the gradient dotted with the step. The cosine formula of video 05 shows why the gradient points steepest uphill and why it is perpendicular to the contour lines.

  • Gradient descent: take a small step against the gradient of the loss, again and again, and walk down the hill. (A "molecule" built from these atoms, previewed here.)
  • One entry per weight: each partial derivative answers "if I nudge only this weight, how fast does the loss change?"
  • Automatic gradients: libraries like PyTorch compute the gradient and store it with the same shape as each weight matrix.
  • Gradient clipping (video 06): measure the gradient's norm and scale it down if it is too large.

Q1. Take \(f(x, y) = 3x + y^2\). What are the two partial derivatives, and what is the gradient at \((2, 1)\)?

Show the answer

Freeze y: \(3x\) plus a constant has slope 3. Freeze x: \(y^2\) has slope \(2y\). At \((2, 1)\), \(2y = 2\). So \(\nabla f(2, 1) = (3, 2)\).

Q2. On the bowl \(x^2 + y^2\) at \((0, 5)\): what is the gradient? Which direction is the steepest way up? In which direction does the height not change?

Show the answer

\(\nabla f = (2x, 2y) = (0, 10)\). The steepest way up is straight in the y direction, with slope 10. The height does not change if you walk in the x direction, along the circle of radius 5. That direction is perpendicular to the gradient.

Q3 (new). On the bowl at \((1, 1)\), take one step against the gradient with step size 0.25. Where do you land, and what is the new height?

Show the answer

\(\nabla f(1, 1) = (2, 2)\). New point: \((1, 1) - 0.25 \times (2, 2) = (0.5, 0.5)\). Height: \(0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5\), down from \(1 + 1 = 2\).

09

Chain rule

When functions are chained, their slopes multiply: the outer slope (at the inner value) times the inner slope.

Needs: video 07 (derivative) and video 08 (gradient) · Axiom: C — local linearity (used twice)

1 · The big idea

A neural network is a long chain of functions. The input goes into layer one. Its output goes into layer two. This continues until the loss, the single number that says how wrong the network is. To train the network, we need the slope of the loss with respect to a weight deep inside that chain. The tool for this is the chain rule. Its message is short: slopes multiply.

Think of a pipeline of machines. The first machine, \(f\), takes \(x\) and gives \(u\). The second machine, \(g\), takes \(u\) and gives \(y\). Putting one function after another like this is called composition, written \(g(f(x))\). Now think of three gears. One turn of the first gear makes three turns of the second gear. One turn of the second gear makes two turns of the third gear. So the third gear turns \(3 \times 2 = 6\) times as fast as the first. The rates multiply. They do not add.

Functions behave the same way. Close up, each machine just multiplies a small change by its own slope. Two multiplications in a row are one multiplication by the product. That is the whole chain rule. Backpropagation, the method every deep learning library uses to train networks, is this rule applied from the end of the network backward.

2 · The formal definition

If \(u = f(x)\) and \(y = g(u)\), the slope of the whole pipeline is:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} \;=\; \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx} \;=\; g'\big(f(x)\big)\cdot f'(x) $$
\(\frac{dy}{dx}\)
"d y by d x" — the slope of the output \(y\) with respect to the input \(x\): how fast \(y\) changes when you nudge \(x\).
\(\frac{dy}{du}\)
"d y by d u" — the slope of the outer machine \(g\) with respect to its own input \(u\).
\(\frac{du}{dx}\)
"d u by d x" — the slope of the inner machine \(f\). The \(du\) parts look like fractions that cancel; that is a good memory aid.
\(g'(f(x))\)
"g prime of f of x" — the outer slope, evaluated at the inner value \(u = f(x)\), not at \(x\).
\(f'(x)\)
"f prime of x" — the inner slope at \(x\).

Why it is true (local linearity, axiom C, used twice). Nudge \(x\) by a small step \(h\). The inner function changes by about \(k = f'(x)\,h\). The outer function sees its input change by \(k\), so it changes by about \(g'(u)\,k = g'(u)\,f'(x)\,h\). Divide by \(h\) and you get the rule.

When a machine has many inputs and many outputs, one slope is not enough. We collect the partial derivative of every output with respect to every input in a grid. This matrix is the Jacobian:

$$ J_F = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial x_2} \\ \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial x_2} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad J_{G\circ F} = J_G \, J_F $$
\(J_F\)
"J sub F" — the Jacobian of \(F\). One row for each output, one column for each input.
\(\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial x_2}\)
"partial F one by partial x two" — how output 1 changes when only input 2 moves.
\(G\circ F\)
"G after F" — the composition \(G(F(x))\).
\(J_G\,J_F\)
"J G times J F" — a matrix product. Chained Jacobians multiply as matrices. \(J_G\) is evaluated at \(F(x)\).

For a linear mapping \(x \mapsto Ax\), the Jacobian is just the matrix \(A\). So the Jacobian is the local linear mapping of a function: close up, every smooth function acts like a matrix.

3 · Worked example 1

Find the slope of \((3x+1)^2\) at \(x = 1\).

  1. Split it. Inner: \(u = f(x) = 3x + 1\). Outer: \(y = g(u) = u^2\).
  2. Inner value: \(u = 3\cdot 1 + 1 = 4\). Output: \(y = 4^2 = 16\).
  3. Inner slope: \(f'(x) = 3\).
  4. Outer slope: \(g'(u) = 2u\). Evaluate it at the inner value \(u = 4\): \(2 \cdot 4 = 8\).
  5. Multiply: \(8 \times 3 = 24\).
  6. Check by expanding: \((3x+1)^2 = 9x^2 + 6x + 1\). Its derivative is \(18x + 6\). At \(x=1\): \(18 + 6 = 24\). It agrees.

A longer chain. Let \(a = 2x\), \(b = a^2\), \(c = 5b\), at \(x = 1\). Then \(a = 2\). The local slopes are \(\frac{da}{dx} = 2\), \(\frac{db}{da} = 2a = 4\), \(\frac{dc}{db} = 5\). Multiply: \(2 \times 4 \times 5 = 40\). Check: \(c = 5(2x)^2 = 20x^2\), with derivative \(40x = 40\) at \(x=1\).

4 · Worked example 2

An ML example: the slope of a loss with respect to a weight. (Our own toy example.) A tiny model predicts \(\hat y = w\,x\). The loss is the squared error \(L = (\hat y - t)^2\). Take the input \(x = 2\), the target \(t = 1\), and the weight \(w = 1.5\).

  1. Forward pass: \(\hat y = 1.5 \times 2 = 3\). Error: \(3 - 1 = 2\). Loss: \(2^2 = 4\).
  2. Outer slope: \(\frac{dL}{d\hat y} = 2(\hat y - t) = 2 \times 2 = 4\).
  3. Inner slope: \(\frac{d\hat y}{dw} = x = 2\).
  4. Chain rule: \(\frac{dL}{dw} = 4 \times 2 = 8\).
  5. Check: \(L(w) = (2w - 1)^2\), so \(L'(w) = 2(2w-1)\cdot 2 = 4(2w - 1) = 4 \times 2 = 8\).

The slope is positive, so gradient descent will make \(w\) smaller. That moves \(\hat y\) toward the target 1.

A Jacobian chain (from the video). \(F(x_1, x_2) = (x_1^2,\; x_1 x_2)\) and \(G(u_1, u_2) = u_1 + u_2^2\), at the point \((1, 2)\).

  1. \(J_F = \begin{bmatrix} 2x_1 & 0 \\ x_2 & x_1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\).
  2. \(F(1,2) = (1, 2)\). \(J_G = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2u_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\).
  3. \(J_G J_F = \begin{bmatrix} 1\cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 2 & 1 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\).
  4. Check: \(G(F(x)) = x_1^2 + x_1^2 x_2^2\). The partials are \(2x_1 + 2x_1 x_2^2 = 2 + 8 = 10\) and \(2x_1^2 x_2 = 4\).

5 · Common mistakes

Evaluating the outer slope at \(x\). For \((3x+1)^2\) at \(x=1\), the outer slope \(2u\) must use \(u = 4\), giving 8. Using \(x = 1\) gives 2 and a wrong answer of 6. The outer machine only ever sees \(u\).

Mixing up the order. \(g(f(x)) = (3x+1)^2\) is 16 at \(x = 1\). But \(f(g(x)) = 3x^2 + 1\) is 4. They are different functions. Always read a composition from the inside out.

Adding instead of multiplying. Like the gears, rates in a chain multiply: \(3 \times 2 = 6\), not \(3 + 2 = 5\).

6 · Where it comes from & where ML uses it

The chain rule comes from local linearity (axiom C): close up, each smooth function is a multiplication by its slope. Backpropagation is a "molecule" built from this atom. It starts at the end, where the slope of the loss with respect to itself is 1. Then it walks backward and multiplies by each local slope. In the three-step chain: \(1\), then \(\times 5 = 5\), then \(\times 4 = 20\), then \(\times 2 = 40\). Each product is computed once and reused for everything further back. That makes the backward pass fast, even with millions of weights.

  • Backpropagation: the slope of the loss for every weight in a deep network.
  • Autograd libraries such as PyTorch: they multiply local slopes (Jacobians) automatically.
  • Layer by layer: each layer is a Jacobian; a deep network multiplies many of them.
  • Loss functions: with the slopes of exp and log (video 10), the chain rule differentiates most ML losses.

1 · The big idea

A computer answers: exactly zero. The model has learned something, but the computer cannot see it. Two functions fix this problem: the exponential and the logarithm. They are a pair. One undoes the other.

    
The exponential is repeated growth. A bacterium splits in two every hour: 2, 4, 8, … After three hours you have \(2^3 = 8\). The power counts how many times we multiply. Now use a special base, \(e \approx 2.718\). It comes from growth that is paid all the time: one euro growing 100% per year becomes 2 if paid once, about 2.61 if paid monthly, about 2.71 if paid daily, and it approaches \(e\) if paid continuously.

    
The logarithm turns the question around: what power gives this number? Its best rule is that it turns products into sums. Before computers, people used printed log tables to multiply long numbers by adding. Machine learning uses the same trick today. Logs turn a fragile product of tiny probabilities into a stable sum of normal negative numbers.


    
2 · The formal definition

    $$ \ln y = x \iff e^{x} = y \qquad (y \gt 0) $$
    

      
\(e\)
"e" — a fixed number, about 2.718. The base of natural growth.

      
\(e^{x}\)
"e to the x" — the exponential. It is 1 at \(x=0\), \(e\) at \(x=1\), about 7.39 at \(x=2\). It is always positive and never reaches zero.

      
\(\ln y\)
"natural log of y" (say "L N of y") — the logarithm: the power you must put on \(e\) to get \(y\).

      
\(\iff\)
"if and only if" — the two statements say the same thing.

      
\(y \gt 0\)
"y greater than zero" — the log exists only for positive numbers, because \(e^x\) is never zero or negative.

    

    
So \(\ln(e^x) = x\): the log undoes the exponential. On a graph, the log curve is the mirror image of the exponential curve across the line \(y = x\). The key rules are:

    $$ e^{a+b} = e^{a}\,e^{b}, \qquad \ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b, \qquad \ln(a^{n}) = n\ln a, \qquad \ln\frac{a}{b} = \ln a - \ln b $$
    

      
\(e^{a+b} = e^a e^b\)
"e to the a plus b equals e to the a times e to the b" — sums in the power become products. \(e^2\) is two copies of \(e\), \(e^3\) is three; together, five copies.

      
\(\ln(ab)\)
"log of a times b" — products become sums. Proof: write \(a = e^p\), \(b = e^q\); then \(ab = e^{p+q}\), whose log is \(p + q\).

      
\(n \ln a\)
"n times log a" — a power is repeated multiplication, so its log is repeated addition.

      
\(\ln a - \ln b\)
"log a minus log b" — division becomes subtraction.

    

    
The slopes (derivatives, from video 07) are very simple:

    $$ \frac{d}{dx}e^{x} = e^{x}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x} $$
    

      
\(\frac{d}{dx}e^x\)
"the derivative of e to the x" — it is itself. At \(x=0\), height 1 and slope 1; at \(x = 1\), height and slope are both \(e\).

      
\(\frac{1}{x}\)
"one over x" — the slope of the log. At \(x=1\) it is 1; at \(x = 4\) it is 1/4; at \(x = 0.01\) it is 100. Near zero the slope is huge.

    


    
3 · Worked example 1

    

      
Use the rules with known logs: \(\ln 2 \approx 0.693\) and \(\ln 3 \approx 1.099\).

      
    
        
  1. \(\ln 6 = \ln(2\cdot 3) = 0.693 + 1.099 = 1.792\). Computing \(\ln 6\) directly also gives 1.792.
    2. 
        
  2. \(\ln 8 = \ln(2^3) = 3 \times 0.693 = 2.079\).
    3. 
        
  3. \(\ln 1.5 = \ln(3/2) = 1.099 - 0.693 = 0.406\).
    4. 
        
  4. Exponential rule: \(e^2 \times e^3 \approx 7.389 \times 20.086 = 148.41\), and \(e^5 \approx 148.41\).
    5. 
      

      
Logs of probabilities. A probability is between 0 and 1, so its log is zero or negative:

      
    
        
  • \(\ln 1 = 0\), \(\ln 0.5 \approx -0.69\), \(\ln 0.01 \approx -4.61\), \(\ln 0.0001 \approx -9.21\).
    • 
        
  • Each time the probability gets 10 times smaller, the log drops by the same step, \(\ln 10 \approx 2.30\). The log is like a ruler that counts zeros.

4 · Worked example 2

    

      
ML use 1 — making scores positive (softmax). A classifier outputs scores 2, 1, and −1. Scores can be negative, but probabilities must be positive.

      
    
        
  1. Apply the exponential: \(e^2 \approx 7.389\), \(e^1 \approx 2.718\), \(e^{-1} \approx 0.368\). All positive.
    2. 
        
  2. Total: \(7.389 + 2.718 + 0.368 = 10.475\).
    3. 
        
  3. Divide by the total: \(7.389/10.475 \approx 0.705\), \(2.718/10.475 \approx 0.259\), \(0.368/10.475 \approx 0.035\).
    4. 
        
  4. They add up to 1 (up to rounding: \(0.705 + 0.259 + 0.035 = 0.999\)). This recipe is called softmax.
    5. 
      

      
ML use 2 — the log-likelihood. Three examples get probabilities 0.9, 0.8, and 0.5.

      
    
        
  1. Product: \(0.9 \times 0.8 \times 0.5 = 0.36\).
    2. 
        
  2. Logs: \(\ln 0.9 \approx -0.105\), \(\ln 0.8 \approx -0.223\), \(\ln 0.5 \approx -0.693\).
    3. 
        
  3. Sum: \(-0.105 - 0.223 - 0.693 = -1.021\) (the video, with more digits, says −1.022).
    4. 
        
  4. Check: \(\ln 0.36 \approx -1.022\). The sum of logs is the log of the product.
    5. 
      

      
Training maximizes this sum of logs instead of the fragile product. Code prefers to minimize, so it flips the sign and takes the average: that is the cross-entropy loss (video 15).

    


    
5 · Common mistakes

    

      
Taking the log of zero or a negative number. No power of \(e\) gives 0 or a negative number, so \(\ln 0\) and \(\ln(-1)\) do not exist. In code, \(\ln 0\) returns minus infinity and breaks training.

      
Multiplying many probabilities directly. A standard computer number (float64) cannot store positive numbers smaller than about \(10^{-324}\); smaller results round to zero. This is called underflow. With 0.01 per factor, the product hits zero after only 162 factors. Add logs instead: \(1000 \times (-4.61) \approx -4605\), a normal number.

      
Worrying that logs change the winner. They do not. A bigger probability always has a bigger log, so the best answer stays the best answer.

    


    
6 · Where it comes from & where ML uses it

    
exp and log are not one of the five axioms. They are school math. But with the chain rule (video 09), the two slopes \(e^x\) and \(1/x\) are enough to differentiate most ML loss functions. The \(1/x\) slope has a learning meaning too: a tiny probability on the right answer gives a big slope, so the model is pushed hard.

    
    
      
  • Softmax: exp turns any scores into positive numbers, then we divide by the total.
    • 
      
  • Log-likelihood: a sum of logs replaces a product of probabilities.
    • 
      
  • Cross-entropy loss: the negative average log-probability of the right answer (video 15).
    • 
      
  • Numerical stability: libraries compute the log directly from the scores, never forming the tiny probability.

1 · The big idea

    
Will this customer click on the ad? Is this email spam? What word will a language model write next? Machine learning lives with uncertainty. We cannot know the answer before it happens. But to compute with uncertainty, we first need numbers. The tool that turns chance into numbers is the random variable.

    
Start with an experiment: anything with an uncertain result, like flipping a coin, rolling a die, or picking a random email. Each possible result is an outcome. Outcomes are often not numbers. "Heads" is a word. An email is a text. Even for two dice, an outcome is a pair, like (blue 3, green 4). Mathematics and computers want numbers, so we need a bridge from outcomes to numbers.

    
That bridge is the random variable: a rule that reads an outcome and gives back one number. Think of a cashier at a market. The basket of fruit is the outcome. The cashier's rule turns the basket into one number, the price. The basket is random; the rule is fixed. A fun fact from the video: a random variable is neither random nor a variable. It is a function. The randomness lives in the outcome, not in the rule.


    
2 · The formal definition

    $$ X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}, \qquad \omega \mapsto X(\omega) $$
    

      
\(X\)
"capital X" — the random variable, the rule. Capital letters name the rule.

      
\(\Omega\)
"omega" (capital) — the set of all outcomes. For two dice it has 36 outcomes. (This symbol is standard; the video simply says "all outcomes".)

      
\(\to \mathbb{R}\)
"to the real numbers" — every outcome is sent to a number.

      
\(\omega\)
"small omega" — one outcome, for example (blue 3, green 4).

      
\(X(\omega)\)
"X of omega" — the number the rule gives to that outcome: \(X(\text{blue }3, \text{green }4) = 7\) if \(X\) is the sum.

    

    
A small letter, \(x\), names one value the rule can take. An event is a set of outcomes. With equally likely outcomes, its chance comes from counting:

    $$ P(X \ge 10) = \frac{\#\{\omega : X(\omega) \ge 10\}}{\#\Omega} = \frac{6}{36} \approx 0.167 $$
    

      
\(P(X \ge 10)\)
"the probability that X is at least ten" — the chance of the event "sum is 10, 11, or 12".

      
\(\#\{\dots\}\)
"the number of outcomes such that…" — count the cells of the grid that pass the test.

      
\(\#\Omega\)
"the number of all outcomes" — 36 for two dice.

    

    
Random variables come in two kinds. A discrete random variable takes separate values you can list: 0 or 1 for a coin, 2 to 12 for the dice sum, the number of emails in a day. A continuous random variable can take any value in a range: a height like 172.3 cm, or a waiting time of 2.51 minutes. For continuous ones, we ask about ranges, like "between 2 and 5 minutes".


    
3 · Worked example 1

    

      
Two dice, three random variables. Roll a blue die and a green die: \(6 \times 6 = 36\) outcomes.

      
    
        
  1. Sum \(X\): blue 3, green 4 gives \(3 + 4 = 7\). Blue 6, green 6 gives 12. Every cell gets a number from 2 to 12.
    2. 
        
  2. The event \(X \ge 10\): sum 10 has 3 cells, (4,6), (5,5), (6,4); sum 11 has 2; sum 12 has 1. Total \(3 + 2 + 1 = 6\). Chance: \(6/36 \approx 0.167\).
    3. 
        
  3. The event \(X = 7\): the diagonal (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1). That is 6 outcomes, more than any other sum. So 7 is the most common sum.
    4. 
        
  4. Extreme events: \(X = 1\) is impossible (empty, 0 outcomes). \(X \le 12\) always happens (all 36 outcomes).
    5. 
        
  5. Same roll, other rules. For blue 2, green 5: the sum is 7, the largest face is 5, and the difference green − blue is \(5 - 2 = 3\). One outcome, three different numbers.
    6. 
      

      
Extra (our own): let \(M\) be the largest face. \(M = 6\) happens when at least one die shows 6: 6 cells with blue 6, plus 6 with green 6, minus the one cell (6,6) counted twice: \(6 + 6 - 1 = 11\). Chance: \(11/36 \approx 0.306\).

    


    
4 · Worked example 2

    

      
ML: labels, noise, and samples.

      
    
        
  1. A label. Pick a random email. Let \(Y = 1\) if it is spam, \(Y = 0\) if not. This is exactly like the coin rule. A classifier tries to predict \(Y\) from the email.
    2. 
        
  2. Samples. Each run of an experiment gives one outcome, and the rule gives one number: a sample of the random variable. The demo's ten coin flips are \(1,1,1,1,0,1,1,0,1,0\). Count the ones: 7. Share of ones: \(7/10 = 0.7\). If these were spam labels, 70% of this small sample would be spam.
    3. 
        
  3. Noise. A thermometer shows the true temperature plus a small error, called noise. True value 20 °C; readings 20.3 or 19.8. The noise is a continuous random variable, and so is the reading.
    4. 
        
  4. A language model. In the book Build a Large Language Model from Scratch, after "every effort moves you", a small model gave "forward" a probability of about 0.57. In 1000 samples, "forward" appeared 582 times: a share of \(582/1000 = 0.582\), close to 0.57.
    5. 
      

      
A data set is a collection of samples. Each row is one sample of a random vector: several random variables measured together, like age, income, and the label.

    


    
5 · Common mistakes

    

      
Thinking the rule itself is random. The rule is a fixed function. Only the outcome is random. Blue 3, green 4 always gives a sum of 7.

      
Expecting the simulation to match counting exactly. In 10,000 rolls, \(X \ge 10\) happened in 0.1637 of the runs, while counting gives 0.1667. Samples are random; with more runs, the share usually gets closer.

      
Thinking each number has only one outcome. Two different outcomes can give the same number: heads-tails and tails-heads both have one head. That is allowed.

1 · The big idea

    
Show a photo to an image classifier. It does not just answer "cat". It answers: cat 0.9, dog 0.08, fox 0.02. A language model does the same for the next word, over tens of thousands of words. These lists of chances are probability distributions. A distribution is the complete list of the values of a random variable, each with its probability.

    
Think of a budget. You have exactly one unit of "chance" to share among all the values. No value gets a negative share, and the shares must use up the whole budget. Something must happen, so the total chance is one. These two rules hold for every distribution, from dice to language models.

    
Real problems have several random variables at once. A joint table holds the chance of every combination. From it, two rules give us everything else. The sum rule lets us forget a variable. The product rule links "together" and "given". From these, Bayes' theorem follows in three lines. It turns a conditional around.


    
2 · The formal definition

    $$ p(x) \ge 0 \;\text{ for every } x, \qquad \sum_{x} p(x) = 1 $$
    

      
\(p(x)\)
"p of x" — the probability that the random variable takes the value \(x\). For the dice sum, \(p(7) = 6/36\).

      
\(\ge 0\)
"is at least zero" — no probability is negative.

      
\(\sum_x\)
"the sum over all x" — add the probabilities of every value.

      
\(= 1\)
"equals one" — the total chance is one.

    

    
For a continuous random variable we cannot list the values. Instead we draw a curve called a density. Probability is the area under the curve over a range, and the total area is 1. A single exact value has no width, so its probability is zero.

    
With two variables \(x\) and \(y\), the two rules are:

    $$ \text{Sum rule: } p(x) = \sum_{y} p(x, y) \qquad \text{Product rule: } p(x, y) = p(y \mid x)\, p(x) $$
    

      
\(p(x, y)\)
"p of x and y" — the joint probability: both values happen together. One cell of the joint table.

      
\(\sum_y p(x,y)\)
"sum over y of p of x and y" — add across a row to forget \(y\). The result \(p(x)\) is called a marginal.

      
\(p(y \mid x)\)
"p of y given x" — the conditional probability: the chance of \(y\) when we already know \(x\). The bar means "given". It equals \(p(x,y)/p(x)\).

    

    
If knowing \(x\) tells us nothing about \(y\), then \(p(y \mid x) = p(y)\). This is independence, and the product rule becomes \(p(x,y) = p(x)\,p(y)\). The product rule works in both directions, so \(p(y\mid x)\,p(x) = p(x \mid y)\,p(y)\). Divide by \(p(y)\):

    $$ p(x \mid y) = \frac{p(y \mid x)\, p(x)}{p(y)} $$
    

      
\(p(x \mid y)\)
"p of x given y" — what we want: for example, the chance of rain given that we see an umbrella.

      
\(p(y \mid x)\)
"p of y given x" — what we know: the chance of an umbrella given rain.

      
\(p(x),\ p(y)\)
"p of x", "p of y" — the two marginals, from the sum rule.

5 · Common mistakes

    

      
Asking a density for one exact value. For a continuous variable, a single value like exactly 170.000 cm has no width, so its probability is zero. Always ask about a range, and compute the area.

      
Mixing up \(p(y\mid x)\) and \(p(x\mid y)\). \(p(\text{umbrella}\mid\text{rain}) \approx 0.833\), but \(p(\text{rain}\mid\text{umbrella}) \approx 0.714\). They are different numbers; Bayes converts one into the other.

      
Assuming independence without checking. Multiply the marginals and compare with the joint cell. For rain and umbrella, 0.105 is far from 0.25.

1 · The big idea

    
A casino does not know who will win tonight. But it knows, almost exactly, how much money it will make over a million games. That long-run number has a name: the expected value. The expected value is the average result you get if you repeat a random experiment many, many times.

    
Start with something you already know: a grade. Your homework score is 80 and your exam score is 90. Homework counts 30% and the exam counts 70%. A plain average gives 85, but that ignores the weights. A weighted average multiplies each score by its weight and adds: 0.3 · 80 + 0.7 · 90 = 24 + 63 = 87. The weights add up to 1, and a big weight pulls the result toward its score.

    
Now the key step. A probability distribution (video 12) is also a list of weights that add up to 1. So we can use the probabilities as weights. Each possible value is weighted by how likely it is. That weighted average is the expected value. In machine learning, a model does not know its error on the next example. But training works with its average error over all examples, and that average is an expected value.


    
2 · The formal definition

    
For a random variable X that takes a list of values, the expected value is:

    $$ \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{x} p(x)\, x $$
    

      
\(\mathbb{E}[X]\)
"E of X" — the expected value of the random variable X: its long-run average.

      
\(\sum_{x}\)
"the sum over all x" — add one term for every value that X can take.

      
\(x\)
"x" — one possible value of X (what can happen).

      
\(p(x)\)
"p of x" — the probability of that value: its weight. All weights add up to 1.

    

    
The expected value is linear. For ordinary numbers a and b:

    $$ \mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y] $$
    

      
\(a,\ b\)
"a", "b" — fixed ordinary numbers (not random).

      
\(X,\ Y\)
"X", "Y" — two random variables. They do not need to be independent.

      
\(aX + bY\)
"a X plus b Y" — a new random variable: scale each one, then add.

    

    
This is axiom A, linearity, again: the expected value respects adding and scaling. Why is it true? E[X] is a sum of probabilities times values. Scaling every value by a scales the whole sum by a. And the expected value of a sum splits into two sums.

    
Finally, the mean of a data set is the sum of the values divided by the count. It is an expected value too: pick one row at random, each with probability 1/N.

    $$ \bar{x} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} x_n $$
    

      
\(\bar{x}\)
"x bar" — the mean of our samples, our best estimate of E[X].

      
\(N\)
"N" — the number of data points (rows).

      
\(x_n\)
"x sub n" — the value in row number n.

    


    
3 · Worked example 1

    

      
A fair die. The values are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. Each has probability 1/6.

      
Step 1. Multiply each value by its probability: 1/6 · 1, 1/6 · 2, …, 1/6 · 6.

      
Step 2. Add them. We can take out the common 1/6: (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) / 6.

      
Step 3. 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 = 21, and 21 / 6 = 3.5.

      
So E[X] = 3.5. You can never roll 3.5! The expected value does not have to be a possible value. It is a balance point: if the six bars were weights on a ruler, the ruler would balance exactly at 3.5.

      
Two dice, with linearity. The expected sum is E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] = 3.5 + 3.5 = 7. No table of 36 outcomes is needed.

      
A new score. Let S = 2X + 1. Then E[S] = 2 · 3.5 + 1 = 7 + 1 = 8.

    


    
4 · Worked example 2

    

      
A toy lottery. A ticket costs 2 euros. With probability 0.1 you win 10 euros, so your net win is 10 − 2 = 8. With probability 0.9 you win nothing, so your net win is −2.

      
Step 1. E[win] = 0.1 · 8 + 0.9 · (−2).

      
Step 2. 0.1 · 8 = 0.8 and 0.9 · (−2) = −1.8.

      
Step 3. 0.8 − 1.8 = −1.

      
On average, every ticket loses 1 euro. One single ticket can still win. But over many tickets you lose about 1 euro per ticket. The expected value tells you the long-run truth, not the result of one game.

    


    
4b · Worked example 3 (machine learning)

    

      
The average loss. For every training example, a model makes an error. We measure it with a number called the loss (video 15). Take four toy examples with losses 0.2, 0.5, 0.1 and 1.2.

      
Step 1. Add the losses: 0.2 + 0.5 + 0.1 + 1.2 = 2.0.

      
Step 2. Divide by the count: 2.0 / 4 = 0.5.

      
Each row has probability 1/4, so this is the same as 1/4 · 0.2 + 1/4 · 0.5 + 1/4 · 0.1 + 1/4 · 1.2 = 0.05 + 0.125 + 0.025 + 0.3 = 0.5. The average loss is an expected value under the "pick a row at random" distribution. It is our estimate of the expected loss on new data.

    


    
5 · Common mistakes

    

      
1. Linearity is not for curves. Linearity works for adding and scaling only. For a curved function such as X², E[X²] is not (E[X])². For a die, E[X²] = (1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36) / 6 = 91 / 6 ≈ 15.17, but (E[X])² = 3.5² = 12.25.

      
2. Expecting the expected value. The expected value is not "the value you will probably see". A die never shows 3.5, and one lottery ticket never loses exactly 1 euro. It is a long-run average.

      
3. Using a plain average when weights differ. In the data set 2, 4, 4, 5, 10, the value 4 appears twice, so it gets the weight 2/5. Big values also pull the mean up: the 10 moves it more than any other row.

    


    
6 · Where it comes from & where ML uses it

    
The expected value is built from axiom A (linearity: it is a weighted sum) and axiom D (the probability rules: the weights are probabilities that add up to 1). The law of large numbers connects it to data: the average of many independent samples gets close to the expected value as the number of samples grows. It never promises an exact match. It promises that big errors become rare. This is why a casino can plan, and why averages over data are reliable.

    
    
      
  • Training objective: training tries to make the average loss over all examples small, not the loss of one example.
    • 
      
  • Mini-batches: because expectation is linear, we can estimate the average loss from small random batches of data.
    • 
      
  • Gradients: linearity lets us take derivatives inside the sum, one example at a time.
    • 
      
  • Data statistics: the mean x̄ of a feature is our estimate of its true long-run average E[X].
    • 
      
  • Variance (video 14): spread is defined as an expected value of squared distances.
    •

1 · The big idea

    
Two classes take the same quiz, and both have an average score of 5 out of 10. Are the two classes the same? Not at all. In class A almost everybody scored 5: the scores are 4, 5, 5, 5, 6. In class B the scores are 2, 4, 5, 6, 8. The average hides this difference. We need a second number that measures spread: how far the values lie from the center.

    
A natural idea is to measure how far each value is from the mean. That signed distance is called a deviation. For class B the deviations are −3, −1, 0, +1, +3. But if you average them, you always get 0. The mean is the balance point, so the deviations on the left and on the right always cancel. The fix is to square each deviation first. A square is never negative, so nothing can cancel. The average of the squared deviations is the variance.

    
With two variables, such as hours studied and quiz score, we can ask a new question: do they move together? Multiply the two deviations of each data point and average them. That is the covariance. Machine learning uses these numbers all the time. We rescale inputs so their spread is 1, and every GPT block computes a spread for each token in layer normalization.


    
2 · The formal definition

    $$ \mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mu)^2\big] $$
    

      
\(\mathrm{Var}(X)\)
"the variance of X" — a number for how spread out the values of X are.

      
\(\mu\)
"mu" — the mean of X, that is, E[X].

      
\(X-\mu\)
"X minus mu" — the deviation: how far a value is from the mean, with its sign.

      
\((\cdot)^2\)
"squared" — makes every deviation positive, so nothing cancels.

      
\(\mathbb{E}[\cdot]\)
"E of" — the expected value: average the squares with their probabilities.

    

    
Read it from the inside: take the deviation, square it, then average. The variance is in squared units (points²). To go back to the original units, take the square root. This is the standard deviation, a typical distance from the mean:

    $$ \sigma = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)} \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mu^2 $$
    

      
\(\sigma\)
"sigma" — the standard deviation, in the same units as the data.

      
\(\mathbb{E}[X^2]\)
"E of X squared" — the mean of the squares.

      
\(\mu^2\)
"mu squared" — the square of the mean. The shortcut: mean of the squares minus square of the mean.

    

    $$ \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\big] $$
    

      
\(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\)
"the covariance of X and Y" — positive: they move together; negative: opposite directions; near zero: no straight-line pattern.

      
\(\mu_X,\ \mu_Y\)
"mu sub X", "mu sub Y" — the mean of X and the mean of Y.

      
\((X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\)
the product of the two deviations. Both on the same side of their means gives a positive product.

    

    $$ \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \mathrm{Var}(X) & \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) \\ \mathrm{Cov}(Y,X) & \mathrm{Var}(Y) \end{pmatrix} $$
    

      
\(\Sigma\)
"capital sigma" — the covariance matrix. Variances on the diagonal, covariances off the diagonal.

      
\(\mathrm{Cov}(Y,X)\)
equals Cov(X, Y), so the matrix is symmetric. With 10 features it would be 10 × 10.

    


    
3 · Worked example 1

    

      
Variance of class B: 2, 4, 5, 6, 8.

      
Step 1. Mean: 2 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 8 = 25, and 25 / 5 = 5.

      
Step 2. Deviations: 2 − 5 = −3, 4 − 5 = −1, 0, +1, +3. They add up to 0, as always.

      
Step 3. Collect the deviations in a vector \(d=(-3,-1,0,1,3)\). The variance is the scaled squared length \(\tfrac1n\,d^\top d\):

      $$ \text{Var}=\tfrac15\begin{bmatrix}-3&-1&0&1&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-3\\-1\\0\\1\\3\end{bmatrix}=\tfrac{9+1+0+1+9}{5}=\tfrac{20}{5}=4 $$
      
Step 5. Standard deviation: σ = √4 = 2 points.

      
Class A: 4, 5, 5, 5, 6. Deviations −1, 0, 0, 0, 1. Squares 1, 0, 0, 0, 1, sum 2. Variance 2 / 5 = 0.4, and σ = √0.4 ≈ 0.63. Class B has ten times more variance.

      
Check with the shortcut. Squares of the scores of B: 4, 16, 25, 36, 64. Sum: 145. Mean of the squares: 145 / 5 = 29. Square of the mean: 5² = 25. 29 − 25 = 4. The same variance.

    


    
4 · Worked example 2

    

      
Covariance of hours and scores. Four students studied x = 1, 3, 5, 7 hours. Their scores were y = 2, 3, 6, 5.

      
Step 1. Means: x̄ = 16 / 4 = 4 and ȳ = 16 / 4 = 4.

      
Step 2. x deviations: −3, −1, 1, 3. y deviations: −2, −1, 2, 1.

      
Step 3. Products: (−3)(−2) = 6, (−1)(−1) = 1, (1)(2) = 2, (3)(1) = 3. Sum: 12.

      
Step 4. Divide by 4: Cov(x, y) = 3. Positive, so more hours go with higher scores in this toy data.

      
The covariance matrix collects both variances and the covariance in one symmetric matrix:

      $$ \Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Var}(x)&\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)\\\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)&\operatorname{Var}(y)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5&3\\3&2.5\end{bmatrix} $$
      
A dot product view. With deviation vectors \(d_x,d_y\), covariance is a scaled dot product \(\tfrac1n\,d_x^\top d_y\):

      $$ \operatorname{Cov}(x,y)=\tfrac14\begin{bmatrix}-3&-1&1&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-2\\-1\\2\\1\end{bmatrix}=\tfrac{6+1+2+3}{4}=\tfrac{12}{4}=3 $$
      
Variance is the same with a vector and itself (\(\tfrac1n\,d^\top d\)): an average squared length.

    


    
4b · Worked example 3 (machine learning)

    

      
Standardization. For each value, subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation. The result is a z-score. For class B, μ = 5 and σ = 2:

      
(2 − 5)/2 = −1.5, (4 − 5)/2 = −0.5, (5 − 5)/2 = 0, (6 − 5)/2 = 0.5, (8 − 5)/2 = 1.5.

      
Check: the squares are 2.25, 0.25, 0, 0.25, 2.25. Sum: 5. Divide by 5: variance 1. The mean is 0. Features in meters, dollars or years now share one scale.

    


    
5 · Common mistakes

    

      
1. Forgetting the square. The plain average of deviations is always 0, so it tells you nothing about spread. Square first, then average.

      
2. "Zero covariance means unrelated." False. Covariance only sees straight-line patterns. Take x = −2, −1, 0, 1, 2 and y = x² = 4, 1, 0, 1, 4. The mean of y is 2. The products x · (y − 2) are −4, 1, 0, −1, 4, which add up to 0. The covariance is 0, but y depends completely on x.

      
3. Dividing by n or by n − 1. We divided by n. For a sample from a larger population, many tools divide by n − 1 instead (Bessel's correction). NumPy's var divides by n by default, but NumPy's cov divides by n − 1. So for the students, np.cov gives 6.667 instead of 5.

    


    
6 · Where it comes from & where ML uses it

    
The mean is a weighted sum (axiom A, linearity) with weights that follow axiom D (the probability rules). Covariance is an average dot product of deviation vectors (axiom B, the inner product). So variance and covariance are built from axioms A, B and D.

    
    
      
  • Standardization: rescale every input feature to mean 0 and variance 1, so many learning methods train more stably.
    • 
      
  • Layer normalization: inside every GPT block, each token's features (768 in GPT-2 small) are standardized. A tiny number ε is added to the variance so the model never divides by zero.
    • 
      
  • PCA (principal component analysis): finds the directions in which the data spreads the most. They come from the covariance matrix.
    • 
      
  • Feature analysis: the sign of a covariance tells you whether two features move together.

A loss function turns "how wrong is this prediction?" into one number that is never negative, and learning means choosing the parameters that make the average loss small.

1 · The big idea

    
How does a model know that it is getting better? It needs a score: one single number that says how wrong it is right now. That number is the loss. Training a model means one thing: change the model so that this number gets smaller.

    
Start with one guess. You guess that your friend is 170 cm tall. In fact, your friend is 180 cm tall. The true value is the label, y. The guess is the prediction, ŷ ("y hat"). A loss function is a rule that takes the label and the prediction and returns a number that is never negative. Zero means a perfect guess. Bigger means worse. Different rules give different loss functions, and the choice of rule decides which mistakes the model cares about most.

    
Even a large language model works this way. In the book Build a Large Language Model, an untrained GPT model starts with a loss near 11 on its training text. After ten passes over the text, the training loss is below 0.4. Every model, from a straight line to GPT, is trained by making some loss smaller.


    
2 · The formal definition

    
Three common loss functions for one example:

    $$ \ell_{\text{sq}}(y,\hat y) = (y-\hat y)^2 \qquad \ell_{\text{abs}}(y,\hat y) = |y-\hat y| \qquad \ell_{0/1}(y,\hat y) = \begin{cases}0 & \hat y = y\\ 1 & \hat y \ne y\end{cases} $$
    

      
\(\ell\)
"l" (a curly letter l) — the loss of one prediction.

      
\(y\)
"y" — the label, the true value.

      
\(\hat y\)
"y hat" — the prediction of the model.

      
\((y-\hat y)^2\)
the squared error: the gap, squared. It grows fast and punishes big mistakes.

      
\(|y-\hat y|\)
the absolute error: the size of the gap, without its sign. It grows evenly.

      
\(\ell_{0/1}\)
"zero-one loss" — 0 if the prediction is right, 1 if it is wrong. It counts mistakes.

    

    
A model makes one prediction per example. The mean of the losses over the data set is the empirical risk ("empirical" means measured on the data we actually have):

    $$ R = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \ell\big(y_n,\hat y_n\big) $$
    

      
\(R\)
"R" — the empirical risk: the average loss over the data.

      
\(N\)
"N" — the number of examples.

      
\(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\)
"the sum for n from 1 to N" — add the loss of every example.

      
\(\tfrac{1}{N}\)
"one over N" — turns the sum into a mean (video 13).

    

    
A model has parameters: knobs we can turn to change what it predicts. For the line ŷ = w · x, the only knob is the slope w. The loss then becomes a function of the knob, and learning means finding its smallest value:

    $$ L(w) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \big(y_n - w\,x_n\big)^2 \qquad w^{*} = \arg\min_w L(w) $$
    

      
\(w\)
"w" — the parameter (slope) of the line.

      
\(L(w)\)
"L of w" — the mean squared error for that slope: the loss curve.

      
\(\arg\min_w\)
"arg min over w" — the value of w that makes L smallest (not the smallest value itself).

      
\(w^{*}\)
"w star" — the best slope: the fitted model.

    

    
Finding the smallest value of a function is called optimization. This is axiom E: learning equals optimization. It is a choice, not a theorem. Machine learning adopts it, and it works very well.


    
3 · Worked example 1

    

      
One guess. y = 180, ŷ = 170. The gap is 180 − 170 = 10. Squared error: 10² = 100. Absolute error: 10.

      
A data set of three. Collect labels and predictions as vectors \(y=(2,4,6)\) and \(\hat y=(1,4,8)\). The error vector is their difference:

      $$ e=y-\hat y=\begin{bmatrix}2\\4\\6\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}1\\4\\8\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\-2\end{bmatrix} $$
      
Mean squared error is the scaled squared length \(\tfrac1n\|e\|^2=\tfrac1n\,e^\top e\):

      $$ \text{MSE}=\tfrac13\begin{bmatrix}1&0&-2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\-2\end{bmatrix}=\tfrac{1+0+4}{3}=\tfrac53\approx1.67 $$
      
Mean absolute error uses the \(L_1\) norm instead: \(\tfrac1n\|e\|_1=\tfrac{1+0+2}{3}=1\).

    


    
4 · Worked example 2

    

      
Fitting a line \(\hat y = w\,x\) to four toy points \((1,2),(2,5),(3,6),(4,8)\). Stack the inputs and labels as vectors \(x=(1,2,3,4)\), \(y=(2,5,6,8)\); the predictions are the scaled vector \(\hat y = w\,x\), and \(L(w)=\tfrac14\|y-wx\|^2=\tfrac14\,e^\top e\).

      
w = 1 (too flat): \(\hat y=(1,2,3,4)\), \(e=y-\hat y=(1,3,3,4)\), \(L=\tfrac14(1+9+9+16)=\tfrac{35}{4}=8.75\).

      
w = 3 (too steep): \(\hat y=(3,6,9,12)\), \(e=(-1,-1,-3,-4)\), \(L=\tfrac14(1+1+9+16)=\tfrac{27}{4}=6.75\).

      
w = 2 (close): \(\hat y=(2,4,6,8)\), \(e=(0,1,0,0)\), \(L=\tfrac14(0+1+0+0)=0.25\).

      
The demo tried 401 slopes from 0 to 4 in steps of 0.01. The loss curve L(w) has the shape of a bowl. Its bottom is at w = 2.07, with a loss of about 0.217. That slope is the fitted model.

    


    
4b · Worked example 3 (outliers)

    

      
Five toy points: (1, 2), (2, 4), (3, 6), (4, 8), (5, 30). The first four lie exactly on y = 2x. The fifth should be 10, but it was recorded as 30. A point far away from the pattern is called an outlier.

      
At w = 2 the outlier has a gap of 30 − 10 = 20. The absolute error charges 20. The squared error charges 20² = 400, louder than all the other points together.

      
The demo found the best slope for each loss. Squared error: w = 3.82, so the line is pulled far up by one bad point. Absolute error: w = 2, exactly the pattern of the other four points.

    


    
5 · Common mistakes

    

      
1. Training with the zero-one loss. It is honest, but its loss curve is a staircase. In the demo, 701 thresholds gave only 3 different loss values. Between the steps the curve is flat, so the slope is 0 and gives no direction. At the steps there is no slope at all. So we train with a smooth loss and only report the count of mistakes at the end.

      
2. "Squared error is always best." Squared error is smooth and easy to minimize, but sensitive to outliers. Absolute error is robust to outliers, but it has a sharp corner at zero. Choosing the loss is choosing what kind of mistakes you care about.

      
3. Mixing up min and arg min. The minimum is the smallest loss (0.217). The arg min is the parameter that gives it (w = 2.07). The model is the arg min.

    


    
6 · Where it comes from & where ML uses it

    
The loss is built on the mean from video 13, and it brings in the last axiom of the series, axiom E: learning equals optimization. Trying 400 slopes worked for one knob. With a billion knobs, a grid would need far too many tries. Instead we follow the slope downhill, step by step.

    
    
      
  • Regression: mean squared error is the standard loss for predicting numbers.
    • 
      
  • Robust fitting: absolute error when the data may contain outliers.
    • 
      
  • Gradient descent: a molecule that combines the gradient with the loss to move the knobs downhill.
    • 
      
  • Language models: the cross-entropy loss uses the logarithm of the probability of the correct next token.
    • 
      
  • Reporting: accuracy (one minus the zero-one loss) is reported at the end, not used for training.

1 · The big idea

    
A neural network is a stack of layers, and each layer multiplies its input by a matrix. Here is a strange fact. If that is all the layers do, then a network with 100 layers is no smarter than a network with one layer. Matrix multiplication is associative, so any stack of linear layers is just one linear layer in disguise. Linear maps can only stretch, turn, flip and squash space. Stacking them never creates a curve.

    
The fix is a small bend between the layers. A function that is not linear is called a nonlinearity. In a neural network, the nonlinearity applied after a layer is called an activation function. It acts on every number separately. The simplest and most popular one is ReLU, the rectified linear unit: keep positive numbers, and replace negative numbers with zero.

    
Think of a hinge on a ruler. A straight ruler can only be a straight line. Add hinges, and you can bend it into almost any shape. ReLU pieces are those hinges. With enough of them, a network can draw almost any curve. That is why linearity plus a bend gives deep learning.


    
2 · The formal definition

    
Why linear layers collapse:

    $$ W_2\,(W_1\,x) = (W_2 W_1)\,x = W x $$
    

      
\(x\)
"x" — the input vector.

      
\(W_1,\ W_2\)
"W one", "W two" — the matrices of layer 1 and layer 2.

      
\(W = W_2 W_1\)
"W equals W two W one" — one single matrix that does the job of both layers (associativity).

    

    
ReLU and its slope:

    $$ \mathrm{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z) \qquad \mathrm{ReLU}'(z) = \begin{cases} 0 & z < 0 \\ 1 & z > 0 \end{cases} $$
    

      
\(z\)
"z" — one number coming out of a layer.

      
\(\max(0,z)\)
"the maximum of zero and z" — z if z is positive, otherwise 0.

      
\(\mathrm{ReLU}'(z)\)
"ReLU prime of z" — the derivative (slope) of ReLU: 0 on the left, 1 on the right. At exactly 0 there is a corner; libraries such as PyTorch use 0 there.

    

    
Three smooth alternatives:

    $$ \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} \qquad \tanh(z) \qquad \mathrm{GELU}(z) = z\,\Phi(z) $$
    

      
\(\sigma(z)\)
"sigmoid of z" — squashes any number into the range 0 to 1. σ(0) = 0.5. Useful when the output should look like a probability.

      
\(\tanh(z)\)
"tanh of z" (hyperbolic tangent) — the same S shape, but the range is −1 to 1. tanh(0) = 0.

      
\(\Phi(z)\)
"capital phi of z" — the probability that a standard normal random value is below z.

      
\(\mathrm{GELU}(z)\)
"GELU of z" — a smooth version of ReLU. GELU(1) ≈ 0.84, and it dips slightly below zero: GELU(−1) ≈ −0.16.

    


    
3 · Worked example 1

    

      
Two layers collapse into one. Input \(x=(3,-1)\), with \(W_1=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) and \(W_2=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\).

      
Step 1. Layer 1: \(h=W_1x=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}\).

      
Step 2. Layer 2: \(W_2h=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\0\end{bmatrix}\).

      
Step 3. Multiply the matrices first: \(W=W_2W_1=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&4\\1&3\end{bmatrix}\).

      
Step 4. Apply \(W\): \(Wx=\begin{bmatrix}2&4\\1&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\0\end{bmatrix}\). Again \((2,0)\) — one matrix did the job of two layers.

    


    
4 · Worked example 2

    

      
ReLU breaks the shortcut. First, single values: ReLU(−2) = 0, ReLU(−0.5) = 0, ReLU(0) = 0, ReLU(1.5) = 1.5, ReLU(3) = 3. Negative numbers are switched off. Positive numbers pass through unchanged.

      
Now put ReLU between the two layers of example 1.

      
Step 1. Layer 1 gave \(h=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}\).

      
Step 2. ReLU acts on each entry: \(\text{ReLU}(h)=\begin{bmatrix}\text{ReLU}(1)\\\text{ReLU}(-1)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}\).

      
Step 3. Layer 2: \(W_2\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}\).

      
Without ReLU we got (2, 0). The answer changed, so the two layers no longer collapse into one matrix.

    


    
4b · Worked example 3 (bends build curves)

    

      
Draw y = x² between −2 and 2 with ReLU pieces. Each piece is a shifted ReLU times a weight, and each adds one corner:

      
g(x) = 4 − 3·ReLU(x + 2) + 2·ReLU(x + 1) + 2·ReLU(x) + 2·ReLU(x − 1)

      
Slopes: from −2 to −1 the slope is −3. At −1 it becomes −3 + 2 = −1. At 0 it becomes +1. At 1 it becomes +3.

      
Check x = 1: 4 − 3·3 + 2·2 + 2·1 + 2·0 = 4 − 9 + 4 + 2 = 1 = 1². ✓

      
Check x = 0.5 (between two corners): 4 − 3·2.5 + 2·1.5 + 2·0.5 + 0 = 4 − 7.5 + 3 + 1 = 0.5. The curve gives 0.5² = 0.25. The gap is 0.25, the largest gap the demo found with four pieces. With eight pieces it drops to 0.0625.

    


    
5 · Common mistakes

    

      
1. "More linear layers make a stronger model." Not without activations. Any stack of matrices is one matrix, because matrix multiplication is associative.

      
2. Dead ReLU units. A slope of 0 has a dark side. Take a unit with weight 1 and bias −10, and inputs between 0 and 5. Its input to ReLU is always negative, so its output and its slope are always 0. The demo found 0 of 11 inputs with slope 1. The unit gets no learning signal and never changes. Smooth cousins like GELU have a small, but not exactly zero, slope for negative inputs, so the unit can still learn.

      
3. Flat tails of sigmoid and tanh. Both become very flat for large positive or negative inputs, so their slopes become tiny. ReLU's slope of 1 lets the signal pass back without shrinking. This is one reason ReLU replaced them inside hidden layers.

    


    
6 · Where it comes from & where ML uses it

    
The layers come from axiom A, linearity. The activation is the one piece that is deliberately not linear. Linearity alone gives one matrix; linearity plus a bend gives deep learning. An activation has no parameters at all. It adds zero to the parameter count, but it is what makes depth useful.

    
    
      
  • MLP (multilayer perceptron): linear, ReLU, linear, ReLU, linear. Build a Large Language Model uses this pattern with 30 and then 20 hidden units. There is one activation after every linear layer, except the last.
    • 
      
  • Transformers: each GPT-2 block has a feed-forward network: linear, GELU, linear. GELU is the only nonlinear piece in that sub-network.
    • 
      
  • Probabilities: sigmoid turns a score into a number between 0 and 1.
    • 
      
  • Training: backpropagation uses the slope of the activation (0 or 1 for ReLU) at every unit.
    •

"""Activation function demo — real NumPy output shown in the video. Toy numbers only."""
import math
import numpy as np


def relu(z):
    return np.maximum(0, z)


W1 = np.array([[1, 2], [0, 1]])            # toy layer 1
W2 = np.array([[2, 0], [1, 1]])            # toy layer 2
x = np.array([3, -1])
h = W1 @ x                                 # layer 1 output
two = W2 @ h                               # layer 2 after layer 1
W = W2 @ W1                                # the single equivalent matrix
one = W @ x
bent = W2 @ relu(h)                        # with ReLU in between

z = np.array([-2, -0.5, 0, 1.5, 3])
rz = relu(z)


def relu_interp(knots, f):
    """Sum of shifted ReLUs that matches f at the knots (piecewise-linear)."""
    s = np.diff(f(knots)) / np.diff(knots)                 # slope of each piece
    a = np.concatenate([[s[0]], np.diff(s)])               # weight of each ReLU
    g = lambda t: f(knots[0]) + sum(ak * relu(t - k) for ak, k in zip(a, knots[:-1]))
    return a, g


sq = lambda t: t ** 2
t = np.linspace(-2, 2, 4001)
a4, g4 = relu_interp(np.linspace(-2, 2, 5), sq)            # 4 pieces
a8, g8 = relu_interp(np.linspace(-2, 2, 9), sq)            # 8 pieces
err4 = np.max(np.abs(g4(t) - sq(t)))
err8 = np.max(np.abs(g8(t) - sq(t)))

eps = 1e-6                                                  # numeric slopes of ReLU
slopes = [(relu(v + eps) - relu(v - eps)) / (2 * eps) for v in (-2.0, 3.0)]
xs = np.linspace(0, 5, 11)                                  # a "dead" unit: w = 1, b = -10
pre = 1 * xs - 10
alive = int(np.sum(pre > 0))

Phi = lambda v: 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(v / math.sqrt(2)))
gelu = {v: v * Phi(v) for v in (-1.0, 1.0)}
sig0, tanh0 = 1 / (1 + math.exp(0)), math.tanh(0)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("W2 @ (W1 @ x)   =", two)
    print("(W2 @ W1) @ x   =", one, "  W2 @ W1 =", W.tolist())
    print("W2 @ relu(W1 x) =", bent)
    print("relu", z.tolist(), "=", rz.tolist())
    print("4 ReLU weights  =", a4.tolist(), " max error:", round(err4, 4))
    print("8 pieces        : max error", round(err8, 4))
    print("ReLU slope at -2 and 3 =", [round(float(s), 3) for s in slopes])
    print("dead unit: inputs with slope 1 =", alive, "of", len(xs))
    print("GELU(-1), GELU(1) =", round(gelu[-1.0], 4), round(gelu[1.0], 4))
    print("sigmoid(0), tanh(0) =", sig0, tanh0)

In summary
    
      
  • A stack of linear layers is just one linear layer, because matrix multiplication is associative: W₂(W₁x) = (W₂W₁)x.
    • 
      
  • An activation function puts a nonlinearity between the layers. ReLU(z) = max(0, z) keeps positive numbers and turns negative numbers into 0; sigmoid, tanh and GELU are smooth alternatives.
    • 
      
  • Sums of shifted ReLUs can draw curves: more pieces, smaller error.
    • 
      
  • The slope of ReLU is 0 or 1, and units that are always negative can die. MLPs use ReLU, and GPT-2 uses GELU.
